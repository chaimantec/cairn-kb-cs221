## Background: How Percy Liang Got into AI

Asked about his first AI class, Percy says he did his undergraduate degree at MIT and took the undergraduate AI class there, but at that time he was much more interested in theory and algorithms ([≈0:50](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈1:38](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Machine learning was not as pervasive or important as it is now, and he could see limits on how the classical AI techniques could scale. In an NLP class he wrote grammars by hand, "which didn't feel very satisfying". Later in his undergraduate years he did algorithms and then got into machine learning and statistical language processing, in the early 2000s, when it was first taking off. That was the way to combine interesting mathematics with a more scalable algorithmic approach ([≈2:25](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

His first project, in 2005, was essentially what one could call **training a language model**, though not a transformer: it was a [hidden Markov model](hidden-markov-models.md) ([≈3:10](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The idea was the same as today's. Take a large amount of text (then perhaps a hundred million words rather than trillions) and do [maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.md), "the same thing we do now, but with a different architecture and a different learning algorithm". The resulting model clustered words. It automatically found a cluster of city names and a cluster of days of the week, and that was the moment he thought "wow", what one could call **emergent capabilities**, and decided to pursue AI ([≈3:57](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

Did he foresee today's language models? Twenty years ago the idea that training generative models on large amounts of data could be useful was already there ([≈3:57](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈4:44](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). But, speaking for himself and he thinks for most people, no one had the imagination to see that sticking with that idea and carrying it to its logical conclusion would give GPT-3 and GPT-5. The step from interesting word clusters to handling many different tasks was a major leap. Even at OpenAI, he thinks, there was a conviction that this was the path, but whether it would arrive in 2020, 2030 or 2050, no one knew ([≈4:44](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈5:31](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). (The captions garble the word for how the models handle tasks; see the transcript.)

## AI Outlook

### What has changed

The big shift, Percy says, is that AI used to be "a researcher thing": researchers ran experiments and wrote papers. Now everyone on the street is talking about it, there are AI billboards on the 101, and it has become a global phenomenon, as happened with the internet ([≈6:17](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The conversation has broadened from techniques and principles to the data, energy, compute and resources of Rishi's lecture ([lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md)), and to jobs. AI "has kind of escaped the lab" and is having real, substantial impact: national policies are written about it, and the biggest companies call themselves AI companies ([≈7:04](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

Research still continues, in his group, at Stanford, at other universities and in industry, and there are still many open problems and things that could be a lot better. That has always been the case and always will be. What is new is "this off-ramp, if you will, from the research highway": a place where research can have impact here and now instead of always looking to the future ([≈7:49](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### Public perception

Much of the public perception of AI, he thinks, has been shaped by science fiction, the Terminator and the like ([≈8:36](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It is also cultural: in the US and the West the outlook is darker and bleaker, while in Asia it is much more optimistic, "a technology that really can transform" ([≈8:36](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈9:22](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The science-fiction baggage anchors people on a sentient agent that comes and destroys. Things could be better or worse than people expect, but in very different directions.

His own picture is that **AI is already everywhere**, making decisions in the background ([≈9:22](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈10:08](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Think of it as infrastructure, or as the recommendations you get on your phone. ChatGPT is more user-facing, but the way AI influences our lives is much more pervasive, and much of it may be hidden, compared with "some AI walking through the door", which is really good for movies. The exchange that follows adds that an AI "working on your spreadsheet" does not seem very Hollywood ([≈10:08](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### The most underhyped and overhyped capabilities

**Underhyped: the probabilistic foundations.** For much of their existence, Percy says, [language models](language-models.md) were probability distributions over the next token given the previous tokens, mathematical objects to be studied as such. They have since turned into systems you give an input and get an output from ([≈10:54](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). What is underappreciated is how much that probabilistic view, minimizing perplexity and having strong foundations, enables everything else. People think in terms of [pre-training](pre-training.md) and [post-training](post-training.md), of which Ken's lecture gave a good overview ([lecture 17](17-language-models.md)), and much of what is visible comes from post-training: teaching the model to solve math problems, write code or analyze financial documents ([≈11:40](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). But what makes AI tick, and this is perhaps hidden, is the ability to **predict the next token**. One way to see it is to look at a model's ability to predict the next token as a function of **sequence length**. A good model should understand the context deeply enough to keep lowering its loss as it predicts, even out to a million tokens, and measuring that loss gets at the true underlying capability of a system in a way that does not show up on public leaderboards ([≈11:40](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈12:27](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

**Overhyped: thinking models.** Many people are interested in thinking models and reasoning ([≈12:27](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). When Percy looks at their thinking traces, they seem to be a very long, very rambling sequence with a lot of inefficiency. You do eventually reach the right answer and get good scores, but, he jokes, "man, this must be a scam to get you to generate more tokens" ([≈13:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The conversation adds that even today no one can be sure how the thinking trace works, whether it is just more budget or actually guides the computation, and that a trace can be wrong and still lead to the right answer ([≈13:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Lecture 17 covers the same idea as test-time scaling, and lecture 5 casts test-time compute as search ([lecture 5](05-search-i.md); [lecture 17](17-language-models.md)).

### Is there still a role for academia?

Some students think academia's role is diminishing because universities lack the compute ([≈13:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈13:59](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy answers from twenty years of watching. Academia has always been a very small fraction of what happens in the world, "this kind of forward-thinking, weird group of people who do weird things". Twenty years ago, if you went to Google and said you had a great machine learning algorithm for search, they would show you the exit, because it was understood that these things were not reliable ([≈13:59](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈14:46](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Research has always been in the position of things that are barely working: "that's the definition". Over the last five years the very methods people were working on, with scale, data and compute, started working, and now Google will welcome you in to work on Gemini.

So this is a transition, and it is not that academia is becoming less relevant but that the techniques suddenly became more relevant: in a sense many of them "graduated" from school ([≈15:33](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Fundamental questions remain unsolved by any means, such as how to really generalize well and how to become more data efficient, which should not stop anyone from productionizing the current best. He sees two classes of work for academia:

- **Long-term, blue-sky research** ([≈16:21](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Peers may not see where that is, because everyone is looking at the particular transformer models that are graduating to industry, and while looking there they miss the new opportunities.
- **Problems academia is well incentivized to work on and industry is not** ([≈16:21](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈17:53](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). His group has been studying how much memorized, copyrighted content can be extracted from language models. Labs will not do that work because they are already being sued, so academia, with no skin in the game, is the only party that can (see [copyright and fair use](copyright-and-fair-use.md), and [lecture 18](18-ai-and-society.md)'s memorization figure). **Evaluation** is another: how to evaluate models fairly and expose their problems is harder inside a company because of a conflict of interest, since a company always has to push capabilities and show that its model is great rather than find its flaws.

### Jobs, and what universities should teach

Students worry that AI is already a very good software engineer, faster than the average graduate ([≈17:53](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy points to a slide Rishi showed in the previous lecture: it is true that the number of entry-level software engineers, as the job has been defined by its skill set for the last ten years, is going down ([≈17:53](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈18:39](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). (In [lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md), slide 3 plots software-developer headcount by age group, with the youngest group falling since late 2022.) You cannot expect to do business as usual, take the same CS classes assuming they are not changing, and get a job. This is very much a transition period, in which he as an instructor and others in the department are trying to work out what education and the workplace should look like, jointly.

At a general level he is not concerned that people will not have jobs, but the kind of work will change ([≈18:39](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈19:25](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). His analogy is earlier technologies. Being able to do mechanical computation quickly used to earn you a job; then calculators came out, and you could no longer get a job multiplying numbers all day, but that did not mean there was nothing else to do. Once you get the primitive where "I just have to specify what I want and the AI will just write it", that frees up opportunities to build other things ([≈19:25](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈20:11](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Think of a company's CEO, probably the busiest person there: many people beneath them have more technical depth and skill, but there is still the job, for the CEO and the whole management chain, of directing and deciding what needs to be done.

The shift, in short, is **from doing to figuring out what to do in the first place** ([≈20:11](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈20:59](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). One takeaway: suppose you had a tool that could build any app in five minutes. What would you build? That is not a trivial question. Which app would actually take off or be useful to people is a very deep one, and the people who can answer it well will be the ones who thrive in the new environment ([≈20:59](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈21:45](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### Undervalued application areas

AI has become almost synonymous with frontier models such as Gemini or ChatGPT, through which everyone gets their exposure to it ([≈21:45](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). But the underlying technology, training a foundation model on tons of data and unlocking new capabilities, is "an incredibly general-purpose technology" ([≈22:31](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md); compare [general-purpose technologies](general-purpose-technologies.md)). It has been applied successfully to DNA sequences, and there are many other data sources, such as climate data, time series and satellite imagery, where the same intuition should carry over. We are, he says, just scratching the surface. He encourages students to think beyond "here's yet another AI assistant" to other disciplines, the sciences, physical materials and neuroscience, and where the same techniques could apply ([≈22:31](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈23:18](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### How big will AI's impact be?

In the first live question a student asks whether AI is just a tool or will drastically transform society ([≈23:18](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy answers on Rishi's scale from the previous lecture, the technology of the year, the decade, the century or ever (the technological Richter scale of [lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md); see [economic growth](economic-growth.md)): "century would be my guess" ([≈23:18](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈24:05](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The general-purpose nature is powerful. He compares it with computing, "a huge deal" some 70 years ago, and puts AI at a similar magnitude of unlock.

### The Turing test, and what should replace it

Do current frontier LLMs pass the Turing test, and if so, what is the next goal ([≈24:05](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md))? Percy first questions whether the [Turing test](turing-test.md) is the right measure at all. He mentioned it on the first day ([lecture 1](01-course-overview.md)), and historically it was important in shaping AI's development ([≈24:54](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). But he does not think it is a good measure of progress now, for two reasons: the **imitation** part, and **humans being the benchmark**. We want AI to be far better than humans at certain things, much more reliable for example, so putting humans as the bar "seems a bit shortsighted".

Is there an easy-to-understand replacement? Tests are often **static**: a system enters the room and gets a score. That has problems, because static tests are gameable ([≈25:40](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He prefers a metric in the **online** sense, and proposes one: **how many new scientific discoveries is AI producing?** That is not gameable. If AI cures cancer, invents a new light material or figures out fusion, no one can say it cheated, and "if you cheated somehow and you figured out how to cure cancer, well, good for you" ([≈25:40](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈26:26](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

## The Class and Stanford

### What changed in CS221 this year

Ken notes that CS221 started almost ten years ago and asks what problems this year's redesign was trying to solve ([≈26:26](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy says there have been a few years with large changes, and this was one of them, with several objectives, "in hindsight it was probably a bit ambitious" ([≈27:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

The first was the **societal impact of AI**. When he designed the class from scratch about eleven years ago it was a technical class that did not think much about societal impact, so its structure does not naturally incorporate it ([≈27:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈27:58](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Yet an intro AI class clearly has to confront these issues, so the latter part of the class now dedicates a homework and some lectures to them (among them [lecture 18](18-ai-and-society.md) and [lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md); see [societal impact](societal-impact.md)). It is work in progress, and he would love feedback.

The second was the **executable lecture** format ([≈27:58](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈28:44](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He has always found a disconnect between lectures, where you look at abstract diagrams and think you understand, and the homework, where you try to code it up and realize you do not see how it connects. Walking through code in lecture, which worked well when he taught CS336 (language models from scratch), leaves no ambiguity about which objects are being talked about. This year was a pilot of bringing that format to CS221, and the first time anything is done there are rough edges. He strongly believes iterating on it will produce a much better experience that combines intuitive concepts with grounding in code, so students can see the whole stack ([≈28:44](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈29:31](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Lecture 1 introduces the format ([lecture 1](01-course-overview.md)).

### How the course's topics connect to language models

Eleven years ago, Ken says, there was no "working AI"; now everyone has ChatGPT and Cursor, while the course spends much of its time on search, MDPs, graphical models and logic. How should students connect those to the language-model paradigm ([≈29:31](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md))? Percy's answer is that many of these concepts are quite relevant ([≈30:17](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈31:02](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)):

- **Search.** In [search](search-problems.md) we think about states and actions and explore different possibilities, which is exactly what **test-time compute** with language models is about. You cannot just train a model and be done: given a model, there is an inference-time problem of how to search over different solutions. Solving really hard problems, doing scientific discovery or a data-science problem will require trial and error, "and that's search". [Lecture 5](05-search-i.md) builds this connection in code.
- **MDPs.** [Markov decision processes](markov-decision-processes.md) are very much what happens when you pre-train a language model and then do [reinforcement learning](reinforcement-learning.md) on it: that is basically trying to learn a policy that gives good results ([≈31:02](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Lecture 17 describes RLHF as [policy gradient](policy-gradients.md) ([post-training](post-training.md)).

He then asks, "any other ones I should defend?", and Ken moves on ([≈31:02](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### What an intro AI class is for

Apart from concepts and knowledge, what should an intro AI class prepare students for, now that AI is here and productive ([≈31:02](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈31:48](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md))? Percy has always believed in understanding the fundamentals of how things are built, peeling back the layers until you reach the bottom ([≈31:48](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). If you just want a job, there are many online resources and tutorials for learning ML engineering, and you would be fine. But the point of being at Stanford and having a general education is longer-term thinking: breadth of topics you would never meet in on-the-job learning, and thinking a layer above "I need to get this PyTorch program working". CS221 tries to lay out some of those abstract ideas and ground them in code ([≈32:35](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### CS221 and the other AI classes

How does CS221 compare with other intro AI classes such as CS229, and is it enough for the next-level classes such as CS336 or CS224N ([≈33:21](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md))? The short answer is yes. After CS221 you can certainly take CS229 and CS224N, and many people take them in different orders, because some of the topics are orthogonal. CS336 is a different level of class: it requires not so much knowledge as experience, and some grit, to build the whole language-modeling stack from scratch, but CS221 provides a good foundation ([≈33:21](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈34:07](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### How he chooses research problems

Asked in a live question how he approaches research and comes up with topics ([≈34:07](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)), Percy says it is not easy and he does not automatically know which topics will pan out: it is betting on the future. **Research is about taking bets** ([≈34:55](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Zoom out, look at the world and ask what big hole needs filling. The best research has some vision of what you are trying to do and, at the same time, a concrete first step you can take.

His example is a few years ago, **decentralized training** ([≈34:55](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈35:41](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Current language-model training needs all the compute in one place. Imagine instead that everyone in the room, and everyone in the world, could plug in their own compute to a peer-to-peer network and train a large foundation model. That would change the power dynamics and what it means to train a model, with implications for AI policy. It is an interesting research direction, and the first technical problem is a systems problem.

On **research taste**, he tries to pick problems where he genuinely does not know the answer, on the boundary of what could and could not work, because research is partly about **information gain** ([≈35:41](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈36:28](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It is not about a quick thing that gets a 5% improvement, which in ten years no one will care about unless there is a more generalizable lesson to take away.

## Career, Life and Research Advice

### If he were graduating now

Ken says this section drew the most questions, and opens with what Percy's first job would be if he were graduating from Stanford now ([≈38:00](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy lays out the options: a big research lab building the next generation of AI, a startup, or grad school; law or business school too, though that is not a job ([≈38:49](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). When he graduated he had zero interest in startups, perhaps because he graduated from MIT. Recently he has been more engaged in the startup ecosystem, and there is something really cool about a small set of people working closely together on a problem ([≈39:38](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It need not be a startup, but being very mission-driven, on something that needs more than an individual contribution, seems most interesting to him ([≈40:24](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). And as a researcher, he would want to work on interesting research problems.

### A framework for choosing

Rather than what he would do, he offers a framework ([≈40:24](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈41:12](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Your first job will most likely not be your last: "you're not getting married", it is probably a few years. So **prioritize growth**. The next part of the journey after Stanford is still education, just a different type of learning. To use the RL analogy, heavily prioritize **exploration rather than exploitation** ([≈41:58](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md); see [reinforcement learning](reinforcement-learning.md)). Being with good people you enjoy working with is the most important thing, and everything else is secondary. It could be an academic setting, a startup or a group in a larger company, as long as you are learning what you want to learn. If you have a mission, say to solve the world's energy problems, the choice is much clearer ([≈42:47](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Otherwise, where you would learn the most is his top criterion.

### Feeling behind

Students often feel that if they are not doing some particular thing by sophomore or junior year they are lagging, as if it were a competition ([≈42:47](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈43:32](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Percy understands the pressure of watching people around you do crazy things. But in perspective, many top researchers at labs, CEOs and others who do very well took a very circuitous path, perhaps through a different field altogether: many people transitioned into AI from something completely unrelated ([≈43:32](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He does not think the internship you get in sophomore year will have any meaningful impact. What matters is you: your skills, what you learn and your outlook on the world ([≈44:18](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It becomes very clear when someone has racked up good-sounding names but is less qualified than someone who has done less and is better positioned skill-wise to succeed.

### Skills in a fast-changing world, and how to judge them

Two follow-ups: with AI doing things it could not do six months ago, will junior students' skills be outdated, and how does one evaluate skills rather than a shiny CV ([≈44:18](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈45:04](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md))? One skill that will never go away, almost by definition, is the **ability to learn and adapt** ([≈45:04](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). In a fast-changing world the only thing that matters is how quickly you can adapt: if the model can do your job, how can you learn something new? Hence his emphasis on learning deeply and also quickly ([≈45:49](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

Evaluating skills, how to interview people, is a tough problem ([≈45:49](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). There are the usual technical screens, and work trials to see how people work together. Softer qualities matter too: grit and passion, because the difference between someone who is really into something and someone just doing a job is night and day ([≈46:35](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). And collaboration, working well with others, is very important in any context ([≈46:35](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈47:22](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It is something universities do not train students well for, at least not explicitly, because most work and evaluation is individual, with group projects not the main focus, whereas in the real world everything is collaborative.

Asked for a common piece of career advice he disagrees with, he says he does not get much advice these days, and suggests perhaps the advice that you must apply to some internship or be left behind, "the same answer as before" ([≈47:22](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈48:09](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

### Is there an AI bubble?

"Surely there's a bubble," Percy says ([≈48:09](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). It distorts not just student choices but everything in the world: the investments big companies make and the decisions governments take ([≈48:54](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He would not say that is all bad, because AI is real. The internet revolution is the prime example: there was clearly a bubble, and we know what happened in 1999–2000, and yet the internet was completely transformative; many aspects of our lives, most perhaps, are now digital in some way ([≈48:54](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈49:42](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). There was a period of overpromising and underdelivering, then a recovery. AI has a lot of real things and a lot of bubbly ones, and at some point the bubbly part will be cut out and the real part will continue.

### Live questions

**What he studied.** "A pretty boring answer" ([≈50:28](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)): computer science as an undergraduate, with a math major as well, which overlapped a lot because he was doing theory; then a PhD in computer science when he got into research; and now he is a CS professor. He was fascinated by physics at one point but did not pursue it as much, because building things in software felt very comfortable ([≈51:13](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

**Why are AI companies so non-transparent?** The amount of transparency has really gone down over the last five years. OpenAI used to publish what it was working on and release models, and now there is no information ([≈51:13](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He gives three reasons ([≈52:02](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈52:47](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)):

1. **Competitive advantage**, the usual reason for trade secrets: if I reveal how I did this training, my competitors find out.
2. **Lawsuits.** Companies train on a lot of internet data, some of it possibly dubious, and laying it all out invites lawsuits (compare [copyright and fair use](copyright-and-fair-use.md)).
3. **A more mundane one: it is not a priority.** Companies would probably be fine revealing some things, but digging up the information, writing a report and getting it through approval takes real engineering effort, and their first priority is to race to build the best AI, so no one has time.

The idea behind the **transparency index** (the Foundation Model Transparency Index of [lecture 18](18-ai-and-society.md); see [openness and transparency](openness-and-transparency.md)) was at least to call this out and make people aware of it, as a first step to stimulating more transparency ([≈52:47](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). Over the last two years some companies have moved quite a bit, and his group has engaged with a number of them, but in some areas, such as data, there is no incentive, and it will take regulation to move them ([≈53:32](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).

**How do we get end users to care about the ethics of AI?** Again transparency comes first: end users generally do not think about how their AI is made, and transparency gives them something to point to ([≈53:32](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈54:17](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). The analogy is getting consumers to care about ethically grown, sustainable or organic food: nutrition labels, and advocacy by organizations pushing for such things to be put in front of users, who mostly just want to get their work done. Many people do not even realize what the concerns are: how much human labor goes into these systems, with its wages and working conditions, and how much compute they take and the strain on the environment ([≈55:04](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md); compare [lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md)'s [supply chain](ai-supply-chain.md)). Making people aware of that is a first step.

**Piano.** Asked whether the competitions he won when young influenced things later, Percy says he has not won any piano competitions lately ([≈55:04](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). He has always been active in piano and still plays a bit, and for a long time it was a parallel track, a hobby ([≈55:51](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). A few years ago he had two postdocs who wanted to combine music and AI, and they wrote a number of papers on training **music foundation models**, which for the first time crossed the two parts of his life.

**Getting involved in research at Stanford** ([≈56:38](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). There are official channels, such as the CURIS program students can apply to. There are also unofficial ones: the nice thing about research is that everything is open, so you can see what research is happening and who is working on what. Look at people's websites, find a paper you like, read it, and engage with the professor or the student; his example is emailing Ken to say you read his paper and have some other ideas ([≈56:38](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)–[≈57:25](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)). That is a more bottom-up approach. The class's final project can also be a way into research that continues into something bigger. There is no one-size-fits-all solution ([≈57:25](../raw/transcripts/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)).
