# Lecture 19 — Economics of AI (AI Supply Chains)

Lecture 19 is a guest lecture by **Rishi Bommasani** ([slide 1](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)), given on Mon Dec 1 2025. The course schedule calls it "AI Supply Chains"; the deck is titled "Economics of AI", and it covers both. Percy Liang introduces it as a continuation of [lecture 18](18-ai-and-society.md). Most of us think about "what's in the box": how to make AI, how to design objective functions and algorithms. Since last time the course has been looking at what is outside the box and how it connects to what is inside: the resources used to make AI upstream, and where AI is used downstream. "It's really important for us technologists to appreciate the larger ecosystem" ([≈0:05](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈0:53](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Percy introduces Rishi: he did his PhD at Stanford, finishing last year, and is now a senior research scholar at Stanford HAI. He led the paper that coined the term *foundation models*, takes part in AI policy discussions in the US and the EU, and has lately been studying AI's impact through economics ([≈0:53](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:39](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

The lecture has three parts ([slide 5](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The first is a **dual lens**: to reason about AI's economic impact one has to think about AI as a technology and, in parallel, about the organizations that build and use it. The second is a description of today's **supply chains** for compute, data and distribution. The third is **growth economics**: how AI might affect GDP, whether AI is a *general-purpose technology*, and what three different beliefs about AI imply for growth. Rishi took AI classes as an undergraduate at Cornell and learned how to build AI systems, but not how they affect society and, in particular, the economy, which is what he worked on through most of his PhD ([≈1:39](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈2:27](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

Notation, used only on slide 67: $Y$ is output (GDP, production), $A$ is total factor productivity (TFP), $K$ is capital, $L$ is labor, and $\alpha$ is the capital share, with $0 \lt \alpha \lt 1$.

## What the Economics of AI Asks

"The economics of AI" can mean several things, and the lecture opens with three kinds of question ([≈2:27](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**AI and the whole economy.** AI is already very important to the economy: the top seven AI companies by market capitalization make up over a third of the entire S&P 500. Slide 2 shows the "Mag 7" share rising from under 10% in 2015 to 35.34% by 2025. The broader question is how far the whole economy's future depends on advances in AI and on these companies. A second question follows: within the AI share of the economy, which specific companies will do well and extract most of the value? That is where supply chains come in ([≈3:14](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈4:00](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md); [slide 2](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**AI and jobs.** At the individual level, as many students are about to enter the labor market, the question is how AI is affecting jobs and the future of work. Colleagues at Stanford studied payroll data from ADP, which the lecturer calls the largest payroll company in the world. A key insight that recurs through the lecture is that **payments are a key mechanism for studying the economy**: ADP's data show how firms spend money on workers, so one can see when they spend less on hiring or pay lower wages. After ChatGPT's release in 2022 they found a rapid, steep drop in hiring of junior software developers ([≈4:00](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈5:31](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 3 plots software-developer headcount by age group, normalized to 1.0 at a dashed line in late 2022. The youngest group, ages 22–25, falls to about 0.8 by 2025, while the groups aged 31 and over rise above 1.0 (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025). More generally the field asks how jobs are changing, how fast different jobs are being hired for, and how the bundle of tasks that makes up a job is changing ([≈5:31](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**AI and inequality between workers.** A study by Erik Brynjolfsson's group followed a call center where some workers adopted a generative-AI tool in 2023. The tool's impact was largest for the newest workers and much smaller for those who had been there a while ([≈5:31](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈6:18](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 4 shows the change in log resolutions per hour by tenure at deployment: about 0.38 for agents with 0 months' tenure, falling steadily to about zero for agents with more than 12 months (Brynjolfsson et al. 2023). For more on how AI's effects differ between groups, see [inequality in AI](ai-inequality.md).

## The Dual Lens: Technology and Organizations

To make progress on these questions one has to think about two things in parallel: AI as a class of technology, and the organizations that develop and use it and through which it affects society ([≈6:18](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈7:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Why is the technology alone not enough? Slide 7 gives three reasons ([≈7:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈9:23](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

- **The overall economic impact depends heavily on organizations outside AI.** AI is a general-purpose technology that cuts across economic sectors, and most of the economy is not the tech sector. AI's cumulative impact will come from the behavior of all those other organizations, most of which will choose either to adopt AI in some particular way or not to.
- **The technology alone does not say who wins and who loses.** Its trajectory might tell you that AI matters economically, but not which specific companies will benefit most or be worst off. That is a distributional question, and it needs attention to the specific organizations in play.
- **Even model developers have many choices besides the technology.**

The example is a benchmark ([≈9:23](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈10:56](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 8 shows the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, where Gemini 3 Pro Preview (Google) scores 73, Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic) 70 and GPT-5.1 (OpenAI) 70 at the top. The lecturer says it does not matter which benchmark it is: what to notice is that the three companies at the top score roughly the same. In terms of capabilities this might suggest that the companies, or at least their models, are roughly equal, and more broadly that they can be treated as substitutes for each other in the economy. Slide 9 states that conclusion as "Google = Anthropic = OpenAI". But even if their models really were equally capable, the three companies make many other choices that differentiate the roles they play in the economy ([slide 10](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈10:56](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **when to release** a model and place it on the market;
- **how to price** it, which affects how consumers and enterprises choose to use it;
- **which products to build**, and how to integrate their models vertically into markets downstream of them;
- **who to partner with** among the other actors in the ecosystem and the economy.

So the "reality" on slide 10 is "Google != Anthropic != OpenAI": firms with similar technology can have very different effects on the global economy. That is the reason for studying supply chains. The technology is understood, but there is a lot of other structure in the ecosystem shaping the economy ([≈10:56](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈11:41](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

## Supply Chains

The abstraction is close to the one Percy gave in lecture 18 ([≈11:41](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈12:29](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)): resources go into model development, and applications come out as the products that affect users. The two principal inputs are **data** and **compute**. Foundation models are distributed through a variety of channels, and AI systems are built on top of them that people or enterprises use for some purpose. Slide 12 draws this as a staircase from **upstream** to **downstream**: a data layer and a compute layer feed the foundation model developer layer, which handles data cleaning and annotation, pre-training, fine-tuning, adding safeguards and internal testing. Below it come a host layer (in-house or a cloud provider, by download or API), an application layer, application users and, at the bottom, affected persons. Brackets group these into development, pre-deployment and post-deployment ([slide 12](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

The same supply chain can be described in two parallel ways ([≈12:29](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈13:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Technologically there are specific datasets, so many GPUs, these models, these coding tools, these APIs. Organizationally there are news companies, cloud service companies, AI companies and coding-tool companies. The lecturer encourages thinking about both at once: companies doing things and having relations with each other, and specific technical assets mediating those relations. The supply chain is very complicated, so the lecture picks three regions where both sides can be understood together: compute, data and distribution. The [AI supply chain](ai-supply-chain.md) topic page collects this part of the lecture.

### Compute

For most people in CS, compute means "some NVIDIA GPUs that sit in some data centers", and that is roughly true, but the compute supply chain is considerably more complicated ([≈13:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈14:02](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 15's "AI Semiconductor Value Chain" is, the lecturer says, a somewhat simplified map of semiconductors and chips. The talk is not about semiconductors, so he does not explain all of it. He asks the class to remember two things: many companies play multiple roles in the supply chain (Nvidia does chip design and also develops CUDA and other aspects of chips), and there are a lot of companies ([≈14:02](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈14:49](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md); [slide 15](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The map runs from design (co-designers such as Broadcom and Marvell, chip designers, EDA and IP vendors) through manufacturing (wafer-fab equipment, foundries, packaging) to chips (GPUs and ASICs, inference-focused chips, edge chips).

Three companies matter most ([slide 16](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈14:49](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈16:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **ASML**, in the Netherlands, makes lithography tools, the optical technology needed to fabricate chips; advanced chips need sophisticated lithography. ASML is "pretty much the only game in town": it holds a global monopoly on extreme-UV lithography, and everything that follows in chip development depends on it. Slide 16 gives its valuation as \$400B, the most valuable European company.
- **TSMC**, in Taiwan, manufactures the chips and so is a critical dependency of Nvidia. It is "the top manufacturer of advanced chips", valued at \$1.5T, the most valuable Asian company.
- **Nvidia** has a dominant share of the chips market, valued at \$4.4T, the most valuable company.

When studying supply chains one is often interested in **monopolies or their approximations**. These three companies have an outsized role at their levels of the stack, each holds a very large share of its market, and each is the most valuable company in its part of the world ([≈16:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

Why care? The reasons differ between compute and data ([≈17:06](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈18:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md); [slide 17](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). For compute they are:

- **Supply-chain resilience.** There are bottlenecks where a single player is fully responsible for one layer of the supply chain, and so is an essential dependency for the next layer.
- **Who accrues value from the growth of AI.** Much of the value at the compute level accrues to these specific companies, partly because they are so essential.
- **Geopolitics.** Each of these companies is so important that it features in the geopolitical conversation about AI. Go to DC and talk to people about AI, and TSMC is central to the conversation about US–China competition, both because it is in Taiwan and because it is so essential. Nvidia features in the debate over export controls: which Nvidia chips may be sold from the US to China.

That makes three layers of abstraction: the technology and how chips are built, the individual companies that are very important to the supply chain, and the broader government-level and societal conversations, all connected to how the technology is built and how concentrated chip development is ([≈18:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

One level downstream are the **clouds**. In AI, cloud compute serves two purposes, model training and model inference ([≈18:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈19:25](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Three major clouds, Amazon, Microsoft and Google, have been very large players for a long time, and they shape how models are trained: many frontier labs have partnerships with, or dependencies on, these three. Inference is a more heterogeneous market, with many more fledgling companies doing inference at scale, and that specialized market is still evolving ([≈19:25](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 18 shows cloud market share from Q4 2018 to Q2 2024: Amazon at around 31–34%, Microsoft rising from about 15% to about 24%, and Google from about 7% to about 12%.

Compute is also more than chips ([slide 19](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈20:11](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). It is not enough to put a lot of chips in a data center: you need the data center and its infrastructure, and so land, electricity, water and other resources. A lot of work is going into that part of the supply chain, especially as it becomes a bottleneck in AI development. The lecturer does not have time to cover it. Slide 19 shows satellite imagery of a data-center campus under construction, noted as part of the Anthropic–Amazon "Project Rainier" collaboration.

### Data

The data supply chain looks very different. A much more heterogeneous set of entities is involved, without the concentration seen in compute, partly because data is less capital-intensive to produce than compute ([≈20:11](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈20:56](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). It helps to separate data already within the AI companies from data outside them that somehow has to get in. That gives six ways an AI company acquires data ([slide 21](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈20:56](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈24:03](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Data within the firm.** *Synthetic data* the company produces itself, for example for post-training with reinforcement learning (the slide's example is RLAIF); the lecture sets this aside because the firm can produce it itself. And *usage data* that comes in through existing mechanisms before any model is trained: people using ChatGPT, or users of the firm's other products and services, such as Gmail or Facebook.
- **Data that is public.** *Public datasets* that are openly available and freely accessible, such as SQuAD (built at Stanford) and the Pile, and *web crawling* of the entire web. There may be a specific owner, but firms acquiring this data are generally not negotiating with anyone; they may simply download it.
- **Data that third parties have.** *New data* generated for building models, such as annotations from companies like Scale or Mercor, used for example in post-training. And *old data* generated for some other purpose and owned by a firm, as Reddit owns the data on its platform and the New York Times owns its news articles; these firms can license or sell it to AI companies.

The data from different sources serves different purposes: some is used in post-training for safety, some in general [pre-training](pre-training.md), which relates to how much of it there is and how easily it can be acquired ([≈24:03](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**Web crawling, and the data ecosystem changing in parallel.** Much of the data used to pre-train models comes from crawling the internet, and like compute the data ecosystem is changing in step with AI development ([≈24:03](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈25:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Websites are changing their policies on whether their data can be acquired for training. Over the last few years crawlers have faced more restrictions: the share of websites with no crawling has risen and the share with no restrictions has fallen. The restrictions are also **asymmetric** between companies. Among websites whose robots.txt disallows a specific crawler, OpenAI's crawlers are targeted more often than Anthropic's. This matters because data may be one way models are differentiated, at least in capability ([≈25:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 22 reproduces the figure from Longpre et al. (2023), which runs from 2016 to 2025 with markers for ChatGPT, GPT-4, GPTBot and "G-Ext.". In its robots.txt panel the band of sites with full restrictions is about 3% until late 2023, then grows to roughly a quarter by mid-2024. In its terms-of-service panel the restricted share grows from about 28% in 2017 to about 55% by 2024. Its third panel tracks restrictions on specific crawler agents, ending with OpenAI at 25.9%, Anthropic and Common Crawl at 13.3% each, and Google at 9.8%.

**Pricing depends on how the data was acquired.** AI companies pay very different amounts, under entirely different schemes, depending on the method ([slide 23](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈25:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈27:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). For synthetic data you pay for compute. For usage data you provide a service and, through your terms of service, acquire the user's data in exchange. Public datasets are free, and web crawling is, as slide 23 puts it, "currently free". From annotation companies you pay for labor, individual worker hours. For old data there is a large negotiated transaction, such as acquiring all of the New York Times's or Reddit's data. These differences in the cost of acquiring each kind of data create asymmetries in how firms downstream choose to acquire data and build models.

**The law, and prices it reveals.** Data is entangled with the law: copyright, piracy and the like. Anthropic recently settled one of its copyright lawsuits, which the lecturer calls the largest copyright settlement in history ([slide 24](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈27:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈28:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The settlement **reveals a price**: \$1.5 billion for about 500,000 works, about \$3,000 per work if all of the works are claimed. Aspects of the law imply relationships about prices. (Lecture 18 discusses the copyright questions themselves; see [copyright and fair use](copyright-and-fair-use.md).)

Why does the structure of the data supply chain matter? Slide 25 gives three reasons ([≈28:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈30:55](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Will we run out of data?** This fundamental technical question depends on quantities that technologists usually do not know but could try to estimate. The simplest is the data mixture we currently train on, and at what rates. Beyond that: how fast data is being produced across different sources in the world, and how abundant each source currently is. Many technical debates involve guessing where data will come from, and understanding the current ecosystem, what is available and how fast it is generated, helps.
- **Compliance.** Copyright and piracy were both relevant to the Anthropic lawsuit. Other laws prohibit using certain data, or even retaining it: in many jurisdictions it is prohibited even to have child sexual abuse material (CSAM), let alone train on it. Privacy law includes the GDPR in Europe. To know whether you comply, you need to understand the supply chain, not just the dataset you end up with.
- **Competition.** When some developers can acquire certain data and others cannot, for example when data is exclusive to one entity, does that give them an advantage in the quality of models they can produce? This comes up again in the growth part of the lecture.

### Distribution

The last region is how models are distributed and so used in the economy ([≈30:55](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈33:14](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 27 is a table of levels of access, adapted from Bommasani et al. (2024), each with an example and a traditional software analogy:

| Level of access | What it means | Example | Software analogy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fully closed | Users cannot directly interact with the model at all | Flamingo (Google) | Trading algorithms used by private hedge funds |
| Hosted access | Users can only interact through a specific application or interface | Midjourney | Cloud consumer software (e.g. Google Docs) |
| API access to model | Users can send requests to the model programmatically | Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic) | Cloud-based API (e.g. Squarespace) |
| API access to fine-tuning | Users can fine-tune the model for their specific needs | GPT-4o (OpenAI) | Enterprise software with customisation APIs (e.g. Salesforce) |
| Open-weight | Users can download and run the model locally | Llama 3 (Meta), Mixtral (Mistral) | Proprietary desktop software (e.g. Microsoft Word) |
| Weights, data and code, with use restrictions | Users can download the model and its inference and training code, under licence restrictions | BLOOM (BigScience) | Source-available software (e.g. Unreal Engine) |
| Fully open | Complete freedom to download, use and modify the model, code and data | GPT-NeoX (EleutherAI) | Open source software (e.g. Firefox, Linux) |

At the top, the developer does not distribute its model externally at all. It may build the model into products it distributes, but gives outside actors no access to the model itself. That is not unusual in software, where specific algorithms and systems are kept entirely within the companies that build them and only downstream products and services are exposed. In between are intermediate options used today, such as an API through which you can query the model. These still count as restricted or closed options, and each has its analog in traditional software and opens the door to some applications while keeping it closed to others. The lecturer draws the boundary of the **open** end at whether the model weights are distributed. On the open side the choices are about what else is distributed: whether the licence restricts how the weights can be used, and whether the training data and code are released too. At the extreme are releases reminiscent of traditional open-source software. Model developers today use different strategies across this spectrum ([≈31:43](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈33:14](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Lecture 18 covers the same spectrum from the transparency side; see [openness and transparency](openness-and-transparency.md).

The choice matters because it shapes the downstream supply chain and how models come to affect the economy ([slide 28](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈33:14](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈36:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Vertical control.** The clearest point: the closed end gives a firm more exclusive control over how its models are used. If it keeps the model to itself and builds products and services on top, it can integrate further into downstream markets. On the other hand it may cede opportunities for the models to be used for purposes it is not investing in.
- **Pricing.** Empirically, providers that sell use of their own models through an API, such as OpenAI or Google, tend to charge more on average than the rates for open models served by a variety of parties. When Qwen (Alibaba) releases its weights, many companies can host inference for the model, so there is a more competitive market for its price than on the closed side.
- **Data flows: privacy and security.** When weights are distributed and others run the model locally or through certain cloud providers, there can be stronger protections, or at least a better understanding, of where data goes when the model is used. That matters for AI in highly regulated sectors that need such assurances.
- **Adaptability.** With the weights you can do much more: fine-tune, distill and so on, opening the door to applications that are hard to build on the closed side.

Empirically there is a lot of variation in how open models are used, quite different from the closed side, so the upstream decision about how to release a model shapes the downstream uses ([≈36:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 29 then pauses for questions; none are captioned.

## Growth

The second half moves from describing today's supply chain to asking where things are going, and how to reason about that in a grounded way ([≈36:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈37:04](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 31 lists work from the previous two or three months that the lecturer recommends. The first is a short piece by Bharat Chandar on the current state of knowledge about AI and labor markets, which he says is great for grounding what is and is not known today. The others, by Tom Cunningham, Boaz Barak, Phil Trammell and Pascual Restrepo, are about where things are headed. People hold very different views about that, and there is a lot to learn from the blend of how AI people and economists are thinking about it ([≈37:04](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈37:51](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The slide also names "Folks at Stanford": Erik Brynjolfsson, Chad Jones, Bharat and Phil.

**Micro versus macro.** This part may feel unfamiliar to CS students because it takes a very macroscopic lens ([slide 32](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈37:51](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈39:23](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Microeconomics studies individual decisions, macroeconomics the whole economy. Computer science has a predominantly micro orientation: it tries to make specific decisions better, such as a better model architecture, a better algorithm or system, a specific alignment method or dataset. The macro view of economics reasons about the huge economy as an aggregate, so what follows lacks some of the specificity of concrete decisions.

**Scope.** "AI" here means frontier AI as currently understood, the LLM class of models, and not, say, Waymo, industrial robots or Facebook's recommender systems. "Economics" means aggregate macro outcomes. Out of scope are how the benefits of technology are distributed to particular people or firms, economic policy, and things outside growth and GDP, such as how students use AI, which matters but does not show up in workplace statistics ([slide 33](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈39:23](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈40:08](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The [economic growth](economic-growth.md) and [general-purpose technologies](general-purpose-technologies.md) topic pages collect this half of the lecture.

### How big will AI's impact be?

Before the lecturer's own framework comes a survey of how others think about it, starting informally with **impact**. Impact is unsigned: very bad and very good effects both make a technology more impactful. Growth, by contrast, has a positive connotation ([≈40:08](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈40:55](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**The technological Richter scale.** Nate Silver's recent book has a "technological Richter scale" that ranks technologies on an exponential scale, by analogy with months in a year, years in a decade and so on ([≈40:55](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slides 35–36 show its six levels: technology of the month (e.g. a smartphone app), of the year (the VCR), of the decade (credit cards), of the century (electricity), of the millennium (agriculture), and of the epoch (the rise of humans). It is very informal, but the lecturer still finds it a useful high-level take ([≈41:41](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**Expert forecasts.** The Forecasting Research Institute recently had experts, among them AI professors (including Tatsu at Stanford), economists and superforecasters, forecast how important AI will be. The modal answer is that AI will be the technology of the century; the lecturer does not go into it but encourages reading why they think so ([≈41:41](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈42:28](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 37 ("LEAP, Murphy et al.") is a bar chart of probabilities over the scale's levels: 7.6 for month, 8.2 for year, 22 for decade, 31 for century, 22 for millennium and 9.4 for epoch. The chart prints no "%".

**Specific forecasts, placed on the scale** (slides 38–43; [≈42:28](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Daron Acemoglu**, an economist and last year's Nobel Prize winner, in his paper "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI". The headline is a fairly modest estimate (slide 39 quotes it: increases in TFP and GDP "in the next 10 years that can be upper bounded by 0.55% and 0.90%, respectively"; the lecturer says "0.5 and 0.9" and returns later to what TFP and GDP mean). It captures the view of a number of economists: AI will be important and will show up in GDP, but it is important rather than game-changing.
- **Arvind and Sayash** of Princeton, computer scientists, in their piece "AI as Normal Technology" (slide 40), predict AI to be slightly more impactful on the scale.
- People in Silicon Valley predict more still. **Dario**, the CEO of Anthropic, describes "a country of geniuses in a datacenter" that will dramatically transform society (slide 41 quotes the passage).
- Considering the **sign** as well as the size gives two very different views: **Sam** saying AI might cure cancer with 10 gigawatts of compute, and **Eliezer** saying we might all die because of AGI. Slide 42 forks the scale into "Utopia" and "Doom", with the 10-gigawatt quotation near Utopia and the book *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* (Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares) near Doom.

The point of the survey: it is hard to reason about a technology's impact as it happens in real time, especially with such different understandings of how big and how consequential it is ([≈44:01](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

### Normal, or generic, technologies

To get a handle on the question, the lecturer goes back to Arvind and Sayash's "normal technologies" ([≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). They do not mean normal like a toaster oven, pedestrian or inconsequential. They mean that a certain class of technologies shares some properties, and that a lot can be done to understand those technologies economically. Slide 44 boxes the middle three levels of the scale: technologies of the decade, the century and the millennium. A better word than "normal" is **generic**, and looking up "generic technologies" leads to the paper by Tim Bresnahan, a Stanford professor, and M. Trajtenberg, which uses the more conventional name economists give them: **general-purpose technologies** (GPTs) ([slides 45 and 48](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈45:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). That gives two questions ([slides 46–47](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈45:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

1. Is AI a general-purpose technology, in the formal sense?
2. If so, is AI governed by the principles economists have found in studying general-purpose technologies in the past?

### Is AI a general-purpose technology?

Bresnahan and Trajtenberg give **three conditions**. The foundation-models paper, which Rishi and Percy wrote with many others at Stanford, claimed that foundation models are a general-purpose technology because they satisfy all three: **pervasiveness**, **improvement over time**, and the ability to **spawn complementary innovations** ([slide 49](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), "Bommasani, …, and Liang (2021)"; [≈45:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈46:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**Pervasiveness.** In the formal economic sense a technology is pervasive when it is adopted **across many sectors**, not merely used a lot, as in the everyday sense in which ChatGPT is pervasive ([≈46:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈47:07](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). That cross-sector use is where the "general purpose" comes in. Anthropic's Economic Index observes non-trivial usage across a variety of market sectors, so the lecturer thinks the first condition holds in broad strokes ([≈47:07](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 51 charts ChatGPT's users growing from about 50 million in January 2023 to about 800 million in September 2025. Slide 52 adds usage share by occupation across three economic index reports: computer and mathematical tasks at 37.2%, 39.6% and 36.9%, then arts and media, education, office support, the sciences, business and finance, architecture and engineering, and management.

**Improvements over time.** Bresnahan and Trajtenberg's definition needs two things under this heading: the technology's **capability rising** over time and its **price falling** over time. Both hold for modern AI ([≈47:07](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈47:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Slide 54 is METR's plot of task length at a 50% success rate, measured by how long the tasks take humans: models are becoming more capable over time, as many other benchmarks also show. Slide 55 is Epoch AI's plot of the price, in dollars per million tokens, to reach a given capability level, falling by between $9\times$ and $900\times$ a year depending on the level. The falling price is "the thing that sometimes gets forgotten", the lecturer says, but it is very important ([≈47:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**Complementary innovations.** This is "the heart of the matter" ([≈48:39](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈50:58](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Erik Brynjolfsson's point is that electricity is a big deal because of the complementary innovations built on top of it, like light bulbs, together with the redesign of how organizations operate. Doing all of that together is where a general-purpose technology produces large gains to the economy ([slide 56](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Asked about this, ChatGPT gives a nice synthesis (slide 57). Electricity on its own would bring maybe a small productivity boost and little change in GDP or other macro indicators. Electricity plus redesigned factories, electric motors, night shifts and new appliances brings broad economic transformation: "one invention → hundreds of complementary inventions → economy-wide transformation." Economists have formal, quantitative conditions for when this one is met. As far as the lecturer is aware, the evidence today is not enough to say they are, but informally there are signs AI is on that trajectory ([slide 58](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Technological** complements: downstream applications are being built on top of models, such as coding tools like Cursor.
- **Organizational** complements: organizations are changing their workflows and how they assign tasks to workers. There is, for instance, a whole new class of tasks: verifying the correctness of AI systems' outputs.

Both kinds look necessary for complementary innovation, both seem to be happening, but the evidence is "more uncertain, even if it may be true" ([≈50:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

### The productivity J-curve

Why does it matter whether AI is a GPT? Because something is known about how productivity, and GDP more generally, behave with general-purpose technologies: a **J-curve** ([slide 59](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), Brynjolfsson et al. 2018; [≈50:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈51:45](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). At first there is a trough while organizations learn how to use the technology productively and extract its benefits, and growth follows. Slide 59's chart of "productivity growth mismeasurement" against years since AI adoption falls to about −0.7% around year 10, crosses zero around year 20 and levels off above 1%. This may help explain a tension today: people in the Bay Area usually think models are very capable and should be able to do extraordinary things, yet the empirical effects observed so far are more muted. Throughout the history of general-purpose technologies there has been a period of delay before large productivity gains.

## GDP and Forecasting

How do specific beliefs about AI translate into specific economic projections ([≈51:45](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md))? Slide 60 is one of the most famous plots in growth economics: world GDP over the long run, flat for most of history and then rising ever more steeply. The zoom on slide 61 is even more famous ([≈51:45](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈52:32](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). On a log scale, US GDP per person has grown at a very predictable rate of about **2% per year**, an exponential (Chad Jones 2023). Even though the economy and history are very complicated, the relationship has been extraordinarily predictable over the last 150 years. So the heart of the question is: will AI disrupt this curve or sustain it, and if it disrupts it, in which direction? Will it break from the exponential trajectory ([≈53:18](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md))?

**"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics"** ([slide 62](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), "Solow; Brynjolfsson (1993)"). The Nobel laureate Bob Solow observed that the effects of computing do not show up in productivity statistics. In our experience these technologies are very important, yet in the way growth economics measures things nothing special has happened over the last 20 or 30 years because of computing. If anything, zooming in on the top right of the curve, growth over maybe the last 20 years has been below 2% ([≈53:18](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈54:05](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Part of the reconciliation is that many Internet-era technologies are free, very cheap or subsidized, paid for by other mechanisms such as ads. **GDP is about payments**, so if using Google Search requires no payment, nothing appears on the ledger in the computation of GDP, and through that lens it looks as if nothing happened, even though something consequential happened economically and socially ([slide 63](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈54:05](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈54:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**GDP-B.** One alternative, from Erik Brynjolfsson and colleagues at Stanford, is GDP-B. It tries to measure what economists call **consumer surplus**: you buy a good because you expect to get more value from it than the price you pay, and the surplus is a direct measure of how valuable a technology is ([slide 64](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈54:52](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈55:38](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). It is measured with choice experiments, in which people state how much they would give up, or be willing to pay, for a good. A survey earlier in 2025 found that about **40%** of people use generative AI tools very frequently, and that on average they would have to be paid **\$98 per month** to stop. Multiplying those numbers and scaling by the US population gives an annual consumer surplus of about \$100 billion (slide 64 prints \$97B). Generative AI may be very valuable, and consumers may already know it, without that showing up in traditional GDP accounting, perhaps because the services are cheap or subsidized ([≈56:23](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈57:09](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

### Three ways AI might affect the economy

With that caveat set aside, the rest assumes GDP is the metric and asks what each of three high-level views implies for it ([slide 65](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈57:09](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈59:26](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The views are that AI will (1) make one sector super productive, (2) act as an injection of cheap labor, or (3) generate new ideas. These are not full forecasts, and the lecturer does not say which he believes. The point is that if you think one of them is the main thing happening, the analysis can be completed to say something about GDP.

**1. One sector made super productive.** AI is in theory general-purpose, but on this view most of its benefit is concentrated in particular sectors, such as building better software ([≈57:54](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Economics says less than one might hope ([slide 66](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈59:26](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:01:43](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). If one final-good sector becomes far more productive, GDP grows, since that sector contributes to it, but GDP grows much less than the sector's productivity does. Prices in the sector fall, and even with latent demand and rising consumption the overall effect in that sector is likely to be lower prices. David Autor's example is **illumination**. A hundred years ago there were candles, then electricity and light bulbs. The illumination sector's productivity rose tremendously, the price of lighting fell dramatically, the number of jobs in lighting fell with it, and lighting's share of the economy is now much smaller than it was. A second-order effect is **Baumol's cost disease**: to compete, other sectors have to offer wages comparable to those of the very productive sector. If software becomes super productive while other sectors change little, you get "really great software", but healthcare might become more expensive. Those sectors then dominate the GDP share computation, and the increase in GDP hoped for from AI is much more muted.

**The production function.** For the next two views the lecturer uses one slide of notation: "you don't really need to remember this slide" ([slide 67](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈1:01:43](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:03:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

$$Y = A \cdot K^{\alpha} \cdot L^{1-\alpha}$$

GDP, $Y$, is the output of three inputs. $L$ is **labor**, the hours of people doing things, and maybe also hours of AI doing things. $K$ is **capital**, things like data centers. $A$ is **total factor productivity** (TFP), the efficiency with which capital and labor together are converted into output. The exponent $\alpha$ is the capital share. Not all economists agree that the economy can be described in this particular functional form. Taking it as given, if you could grow only $K$ or only $L$, you would want to grow them at equal rates to grow $Y$ as fast as possible, which the lecturer calls "constant returns of scale" ([≈1:03:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**2. AI as an injection of cheap labor.** This cross-cutting view treats AI broadly as a cheaper form of labor, applicable at different rates in different domains, and asks what injecting a new source of labor supply into the economy does. The lecturer says this is similar to how Acemoglu arrives at his estimates ([≈57:54](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈58:40](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). In the production function, $L$ grows ([slide 68](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈1:03:16](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:05:35](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). If capital $K$ grows roughly in line with it, there is broad GDP growth, possibly a lot of it. Slide 68 says the GDP curve may shift up if the injection is one-time, or its exponential rate may grow if the injection is sustained, assuming capital keeps up. If $L$ rapidly outpaces $K$, the new labor might not translate into anything useful, and GDP might not keep pace. A reason for optimism is the baseline: in developed economies population growth has basically stagnated, and the labor share, the fraction of people alive who work, has plateaued or may even be falling. Under this view what matters is how effective AI is as labor: which specific tasks it can do, how much more efficiently, and how much of this labor AI can create. This is the AI that writes reports, prepares documents or makes medical diagnoses.

**3. AI as a generator of ideas.** If an important function of AI is to produce new ideas, the long-run prediction is more aggressive: an increase in the growth rate, maybe even growth faster than exponential ([slide 69](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈1:05:35](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:07:54](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). This comes from a couple of Nobel laureates, most influentially **Paul Romer**, whose work shows that ideas are the foundation of growth in a technical economic sense. Ideas are special because they are **non-rival**: once invented, an idea can be reused arbitrarily, by many people. Once the concepts of linear algebra existed, many people could reuse them. A traditional good is different: if I make one computer it helps me, but for you to benefit I have to make a second one. (There are nuances: you still have to learn to use an idea.) On this view what matters is not so much how AI affects existing tasks as whether it gives new ways to do them much more efficiently, or creates new ideas and new tasks altogether. So the focus is how AI affects the production of ideas, in R&D and science. For specific models the lecturer points to Ben Jones's work on AI and R&D (slide 69: "See Ben Jones (2025)").

People hold very different views of what will happen at the intersection of AI and the economy, for different reasons. But given any particular view, it is increasingly possible to write out what it implies economically, or at least to reason about it from first principles ([≈1:07:54](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

## Questions

**The lecturer's own forecast** ([≈1:08:40](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:10:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Asked where AI falls on the decade/century/millennium scale and how it might affect GDP, he puts most of his probability on century or decade, and would err towards century. How AI will affect GDP is not clear to him. He is more confident in the second view, that AI's main effect will be cross-cutting, on labor and substitution. Whether AI mainly augments or automates workers in particular jobs and sectors may not matter much for overall GDP, but it matters a great deal for what happens to workers. He also thinks new measurement methods are needed. As an AI person he is interested in AI in particular, and GDP is a very coarse way to see its effects. He would prefer more bespoke tools for studying AI, even if they are not the best tools for studying the economy as a whole, and he is working on some.

**Will natural resources limit AI's growth?** ([≈1:10:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:11:44](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)) AI is testing parts of the supply chain that could long be assumed to just be there and were not rate-limiting, with today's large capital expenditure and data-center build-out. He thinks these factors will matter. His guess is that in the US an important rate-limiting factor is the ability to **transmit** power rather than to **produce** it, which may be why part of the US conversation is about such things. He adds that he does not understand them well enough to say more.

**What made the J-curve dips of earlier general-purpose technologies deep, and what are the analogues now?** ([≈1:11:44](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:14:03](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)) Brynjolfsson's J-curve paper is theoretical: under some assumptions there is a near-term productivity trough before growth. It aligns with intuition and some observations, but the lecturer is not aware of a clean, measured instance where the J has actually been seen for a specific technology in TFP or some other productivity measure. His understanding is that economists group innovation complementarities into two categories. First, you have to build the things downstream of the technology: for electricity, the grids, the outlets, the ways of transmitting electricity through the economy. The slower part is the organizational change needed to use the technology even once those complements exist. On that view the organizational and human factors are the slowest, coming later in the process before productivity gains appear.
