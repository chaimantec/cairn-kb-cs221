# General-Purpose Technologies

A **general-purpose technology** (GPT) is, in the economists' sense, a technology like the steam engine, the electric motor or electricity that drives whole waves of transformation and productivity growth across the economy. [Lecture 19](19-economics-of-ai.md) uses the idea to reason about AI's economic impact. If AI is a GPT, then what economists have learned about past GPTs may apply to it.

## From "normal" to "generic" to "general purpose"

The term enters lecture 19 through Arvind and Sayash's piece "AI as Normal Technology" ([≈43:14](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md); [slide 40](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). "Normal" there does not mean pedestrian or inconsequential, like a toaster oven. It means that a certain class of technologies shares properties that can be studied economically ([≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). On the "technological Richter scale" of slide 44 these are technologies of the decade, the century and the millennium: credit cards, electricity, agriculture. A better word than "normal" is **generic**, and that leads to the paper by Tim Bresnahan, a Stanford professor, and M. Trajtenberg, "General purpose technologies 'Engines of growth'?" ([slides 45 and 48](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈44:48](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈45:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Its abstract, on slide 48, says GPTs are characterized by "pervasiveness, inherent potential for technical improvements, and 'innovational complementarities', giving rise to increasing returns-to-scale".

## The three characteristics

Lecture 19 states the three conditions as **pervasiveness**, **improvement over time** and the ability to **spawn complementary innovations**. The foundation-models report that Rishi Bommasani and Percy Liang wrote with many others at Stanford claimed that foundation models meet all three, and are therefore a general-purpose technology ([slide 49](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), "Bommasani, …, and Liang (2021)"; [≈45:34](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈46:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). The lecture checks each for modern AI.

**Pervasiveness** means adoption **across many sectors** of the economy, not merely heavy use ([≈46:20](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈47:07](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). That is where "general purpose" comes from. ChatGPT is used a lot, but the evidence that matters is that Anthropic's Economic Index finds non-trivial usage across many market sectors. Slide 52 shows usage by occupation category, led by computer and mathematical tasks at about 37–40%. The lecturer concludes that the condition holds in broad strokes ([slides 51–52](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)).

**Improvement over time** has two parts in Bresnahan and Trajtenberg's definition: **capability rising** and **price falling** ([≈47:07](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈48:39](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Both hold. METR's measurements show models completing ever longer tasks, measured by how long they take humans (slide 54). Epoch AI's data show the price of reaching a given capability level falling by between $9\times$ and $900\times$ a year (slide 55). The lecturer stresses that the price half "sometimes gets forgotten".

**Complementary innovations** are "the heart of the matter" ([≈48:39](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈50:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Erik Brynjolfsson's example is electricity ([slide 56](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Electricity by itself brings modest gains. Electricity together with light bulbs, electric motors, redesigned factories, night shifts and new appliances brings an economy-wide transformation ([slide 57](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Economists have quantitative conditions for when this criterion is met, and as far as the lecturer knows the evidence is not yet there for AI. Informally, though, both kinds of complement seem to be appearing ([slide 58](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md); [≈50:12](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)):

- **Technological**: applications built on models, such as coding tools like Cursor.
- **Organizational**: redesigned workflows, including a new class of tasks verifying the correctness of AI outputs.

## The productivity J-curve

What follows if AI is a GPT? One answer is the **J-curve** of productivity ([slide 59](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), Brynjolfsson et al. 2018; [≈50:58](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈51:45](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Organizations need time to learn to use a new GPT productively. Early adoption is messy and costly, so measured productivity first dips, then rises as processes are redesigned, workers trained and complements invented. Slide 59's illustrative curve falls to about −0.7% around ten years after adoption, crosses zero around twenty, and levels off above 1%. This may explain why people in the Bay Area see very capable models while the observed economic effects are still muted.

The J-curve result is theoretical. Asked about the J-curves of earlier technologies, the lecturer says he is not aware of a clean, measured instance of the J in productivity data for a specific technology ([≈1:12:30](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:13:17](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). He gives economists' grouping of complementarities as an explanation of the delay ([≈1:13:17](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)–[≈1:14:03](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). First the downstream infrastructure has to be built: for electricity, grids, outlets and transmission. The slower part is the organizational and human change needed to use the technology even once the infrastructure exists.

## The productivity paradox

Related is Solow's observation, "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" ([slide 62](../raw/slides/19-economics-of-ai.md), "Solow; Brynjolfsson (1993)"; [≈53:18](../raw/transcripts/19-economics-of-ai.md)). Computing feels transformative, yet growth has not visibly accelerated. Part of the answer is that GDP counts payments, and many Internet-era technologies are free. See [economic growth](economic-growth.md) for this and for GDP-B, a measure of the consumer surplus that GDP misses.
