# Lecture 17 — Introduction to Language Models

Lecture 17 opens the course's closing stretch, which zooms out from algorithms to AI more broadly. It was given on Mon Nov 17 2025 by **Ken Liu** ([slide 1](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), rather than by the course's instructor Percy Liang, two days before the exam, and it is the one lecture of the course delivered as a slide deck rather than an executable Python program: "you don't have to trace through the code. We're not going to test you on it. You're just going to sit and enjoy" ([≈0:05](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md); slide 2 says language models will not be in the exam). It is "a gentle introduction for making sense of" today's language models in four parts ([slide 11](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). First, what a [language model](language-models.md) is: a distribution over sequences, seen as tensors and as probabilities, and implemented by counting, by n-grams or by a neural network. Second, why language is worth modeling. Third, what makes language models work: architecture, [pre-training](pre-training.md) and [post-training](post-training.md), [tokenization](tokenization.md) and systems. Fourth, where the field is today. Much of it ties back to earlier lectures: the [tensor](tensors.md) shapes of homework 2, the chain rule and the Markov assumption of the Bayesian network lectures (12–14), the policy gradient of lectures 8–9, and the first-order logic of lectures 15–16.

Notation, as on the slides: $T$ is the sequence length (number of tokens), $D$ the embedding dimension, $V$ the vocabulary size, and $B$ the batch size; tensor shapes are written $(T,)$, $(T, D)$, $(B, T, V)$ and so on. $x_t$ is the token at position $t$ and $x_{1:t-1}$ the tokens before it. On slide 6 alone, the $6ND$ rule uses $N$ for the parameter count and $D$ for the number of training tokens, which is a different $D$ from the embedding dimension.

## Language Models Are Everywhere, and Industrialized

The lecture starts from how common language models have become ([≈0:05](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈0:50](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 3](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)): ChatGPT, the autocomplete on a phone keyboard ("a tiny language model"), code completion in Cursor, and also robotics, shown as a robot arm folding a T-shirt, and voice and video. Because they are so capable they are also being industrialized: big companies build "giant artifacts" and ship them, for example Meta's Llama 3 and Alibaba's Qwen3 ([slide 4](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

The scale is the point of the next few slides, and the lecturer wants the class to grasp it ([≈1:37](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈3:54](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 5–6](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)):

- **Data.** Qwen3 is trained on 36 trillion tokens, roughly 27 trillion words or about 144 TB of raw text. At 300 words a page that is about 90 billion sheets of paper, or about 300 million books; stacked, the sheets would reach about 9,000 km, against the International Space Station's orbit at about 400 km and the Earth's radius of about 6,371 km. Typing it at 50 words a minute would take about a million years.
- **Compute.** Llama 3 trained its 405-billion-parameter model on 16 trillion tokens. The slide's $6ND$ rule estimates the training compute as 6 times the parameter count times the token count, $6 \times (405 \times 10^9) \times (16 \times 10^{12}) \approx 3.9 \times 10^{25}$ FLOPs, against Meta's reported $3.8 \times 10^{25}$.
- **Time and money.** Dividing by the FLOPs one H100 GPU does in a day, $3.8 \times 10^{25} / 4.32 \times 10^{19}$, gives about 880,000 H100-days, or 650,000 years on a MacBook M2. At \$2 per H100-hour that is about \$42 million "for a single pre-training run, assuming no failure."

The build-out continues: the slide shows NVIDIA's stock chart, a Google Research post on Project Suncatcher (satellite constellations carrying TPUs) and NVIDIA's post on Starcloud's H100-powered satellite. "We might just have GPUs in space before Stanford wins the Big Game," which was the next week ([≈3:09](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈3:54](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 7](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The teams are large too: more than 150 contributors for Qwen3, and three pages of author names each for Llama 3 and GPT-4 ([slide 8](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

And all of it buys "some giant matrices of numbers," of the kind the class met in homework 2 and the first lectures ([≈3:54](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). Slide 9 shows the tensor index of a Hugging Face model, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Thinking, as a list of weight names and shapes, for example an embedding matrix of shape [151 936, 4 096]. Slide 10, which the deck credits to Andrej Karpathy, summarizes the whole pipeline with the numbers for Llama 2 70B: a chunk of the internet (about 10 TB of text) goes through 6,000 GPUs for 12 days (about \$2M and $10^{24}$ FLOPs) and comes out as a roughly 140 GB `parameters.zip` ([≈4:42](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

## What Exactly Is a Language Model?

### Language: vocabulary and grammar

"Language models, by definition, are models of language," which on its own is not helpful ([≈5:27](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). For the lecture's purposes, **language is just a (structured) sequence of "characters"** ([slide 13](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), and the lecturer adds that many people who build language models today do not think of language as much more than that. The examples are English, Tamil, Python ("the language that you work with day to day") and sign language ([≈6:14](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). The structure comes from two things ([slide 14](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)): the **vocabulary**, the possible set of characters, and the **grammar**, the rules or conventions for how characters follow each other. Learning a model of a language means learning that structure and learning to best predict or produce it, for instance to say how likely a sequence is under the language ([≈7:00](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

### Completing a sentence

The running example is *The stock market crashed and investors ___* ([≈7:00](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈8:34](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 15–21](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Anyone who speaks English has a belief about the blank:

- **桌子**, which the lecturer glosses as "desk in Chinese," is the wrong vocabulary: it is not an English word.
- **golfing** has the wrong semantics and grammar, but is at least within English.
- **panicked** is plausible.
- **celebrated** is also plausible, for instance if you wanted to buy stocks cheaply. To prefer it, a model would need to know something about the real world, since "panic is more common" in English text.

What a model prefers reflects the words and beliefs it has seen. A language model, then, is an object obtained ("trained") to model or predict language: it takes in a prefix and completes the text. There are other ways to model language, but the lecture sticks to completing text sequentially ([≈9:20](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). Slide 21 draws it: the prefix goes into a box labelled "Language Model" and comes out as probabilities over the next word, 1% for 桌子, 5% for golfing, 64% for panicked and 30% for celebrated.

### The tensor view

To make this concrete ([≈9:20](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈14:41](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 22–33](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), represent each word as a vector, as in homework 2, and take a vocabulary of only 10 words, a Python dictionary from strings to indices ([slide 22](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)):

- The input sentence becomes a vector of **word IDs** (the "input IDs"), of shape $(6,)$ for the six-word prefix.
- An **embedding** turns each ID into a vector of dimension $D$; with $D = 4$ the prefix becomes a $6 \times 4$ matrix ([slide 25](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).
- The model then does **multiclass classification over the vocabulary**: its output is a vector of shape $(10,)$, a probability for each word, which should be high for panicked or celebrated and very low for a word like investors ([≈10:53](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 26](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). This vector represents $P(\text{word} \mid \text{The stock market crashed and investors})$.

In general the sequence length is $T$, the embedding dimension $D$ and the vocabulary size $V$, so the shapes are $(T,)$ in, $(T, D)$ after embedding, and $(V,)$ out: "a V-class classification task essentially" ([≈11:39](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 27](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Since the whole input is there anyway, the model can also **predict at every position**: the output becomes a $(T, V)$ tensor whose first row is the distribution over the word after *The*, the second over the word after *The stock*, and so on ([≈12:24](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 28](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

**Autoregression** ([≈13:10](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈13:56](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 29–32](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Given the distribution at the last position, pick a word: take the most likely one, which is **greedy decoding**, or **sample** at random according to the probabilities. Say it picks *panicked*. Append it to the input, which is now one token longer, and run the model again to get the next distribution, and keep going. Predicting words one after another this way is **autoregressive language modeling**.

**Batching** ([≈13:56](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈14:41](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 33](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A language model is just matrices and tensors, so as long as the dimensions work out it can process many sequences at once: with a batch of $B$ sentences the shapes are $(B, T)$, $(B, T, D)$ and $(B, T, V)$, the standard batching from homework 2. See [tensors](tensors.md) for shapes in general.

The numbers on these slides are illustrative and are not mutually consistent: the word IDs printed under the sentence, $[1, 3, 2, 5, 6, 4]$, do not match slide 22's vocabulary (by which the prefix would be $[0, 5, 4, 2, 1, 3]$), the autoregression slides give *panicked* the ID 9 where slide 22 gives it 7, and slide 26's ten printed probabilities add up to more than 1 ([slide file](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The lecture does not comment on the values.

### The probabilistic view

A language model is just **a distribution over sequences** ([≈14:41](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈16:59](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 34–35](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A good one says how likely each sequence is: say $P(\text{The, stock, market, crashed, and, investors, panicked}) = 0.02$, with *celebrated* at 0.015 and *golfing* at 0.0001. The 2% is a share of all possible sequences ("I ate lunch today", "I didn't go to the gym yesterday"), not only of the completions of this prefix ([≈15:29](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

The joint probability unpacks with the **chain rule**, which the class learned in the Bayesian network lectures:

$$P(\text{The, stock, market, crashed, and, investors, celebrated}) = P(\text{The}) \thinspace P(\text{stock} \mid \text{The}) \thinspace P(\text{market} \mid \text{The stock}) \cdots$$

that is,

$$P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_i P(x_i \mid x_{1:i-1}).$$

So "we only need a model that, given past context, predicts 1 word (token) at a time," which is what *autoregressive* means ([slide 35](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). This is the same factorization [lecture 12](12-bayesian-networks-i.md) drew as a [Bayesian network](bayesian-networks.md) in which every token depends on all earlier ones.

### Objectives: next-token prediction and masked language modeling

There are several objectives for language modeling ([≈16:59](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈18:32](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 36–38](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

1. **Next-word (token) prediction (NTP)** is multiclass classification over the vocabulary, done sequentially: input $(B, T)$, output $(B, T, V)$, learning $P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$. It is the objective used in practice, at least for pre-training, by all the language models the class has seen, and other objectives are rare.
2. **Masked language modeling (MLM)** predicts a missing token in the middle, learning for example $P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}, x_{t+1}, \ldots, x_T)$, an infill. For `import ___ as np`, a word in another script is the wrong vocabulary and `import` the wrong grammar, while `numpy` and `jax.numpy` (JAX's implementation of the same interface) are both plausible. MLM was popularized by BERT in 2018, when people found that pre-training on a lot of text with this objective made models very useful, but it is not used as often today.

### Three ways to implement a language model

Given what a language model is, there are many ways to implement one ([≈19:18](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈23:57](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 39–48](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

**1. Just count.** Gather a big pile of internet documents (news sites, Reddit and so on), count every sequence, and assign each its empirical probability ([≈19:18](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈20:51](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 40–42](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The problem is that the exact string may never show up: in slide 42's four snippets, "the stock market", "market crashed and", "the stock market crashed" and "and investors panicked" all occur, but never the whole sentence, so $P(\text{string}) = 0$ "most of the time, especially with long text!"

**2. Count n-grams.** An **n-gram** is $n$ consecutive words, and an **n-gram language model** approximates each conditional probability using only the previous $n - 1$ words ([≈20:51](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈22:24](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 43–46](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). With $n = 3$,

$$P(\text{panicked} \mid \text{The} \ldots \text{investors}) \approx P(\text{panicked} \mid \text{and, investors}),$$

and likewise for every factor of the chain rule. This is "the Markov assumption but for $n$ instead of 1," as in the Bayesian network lectures (see [hidden Markov models](hidden-markov-models.md)). The same documents now give non-zero counts: "stock market" appears twice, once followed by *crashed*, so $P(\text{crashed} \mid \text{stock, market}) = 1/2$. The model is "essentially a giant lookup table of probabilities." (Estimating a conditional probability by dividing counts is the count-and-normalize estimate of [lecture 14](14-bayesian-networks-learning.md); lecture 17 does not make the connection or discuss smoothing.)

**3. Neural networks.** "We're not here to count text. We're here to train cool AI models" ([≈22:24](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈23:57](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 47–48](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A neural network takes the sequence and predicts the next word, and is trained by [gradient descent](gradient-descent.md). The shapes are as before: input IDs $(T,)$, embeddings $(T, D)$, predictions $(T, V)$. Because it is a giant multiclass classification problem, the labels come for free from the input text itself: **shift it by one position**. The label for the prediction after *The* is *stock*, after *The stock* it is *market*, and so on. That gives a loss, and [backpropagation](backpropagation.md) gives the gradients.

## Why Is It a Good Idea to Model Language?

Language modeling is not a new idea, but three reasons explain why it is such a good one ([slides 49–70](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

**1. Many tasks are just language modeling** ([≈24:42](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈26:16](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 50–53](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Much of what people do day to day is sequence completion. An email to the teaching team, "may I please get an extension for homework 9. I was…", gets the extension if the next words are the right ones, and not if they amount to having been very bored: "the correct next-word distribution (i.e. language model) may get you tangible benefits" ([slide 51](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Writing code is predicting the next token of the code written so far. Replying to your advisor's messages, the example of a friend's research project, is completing a conversation from what they sent and what you know about yourself.

**2. Language modeling enables multi-task learning** ([≈26:16](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈30:51](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 54–62](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Training on many kinds of text with one objective, next-token prediction, teaches the model about many tasks at once:

- To predict the next tokens of the Wikipedia article on Alan Turing (his date of death, where he was born, what he did in which year), the model has to **memorize facts** about the world.
- To predict the next tokens of Wolfram MathWorld's page on irreducible fractions (that $(4 + 7i)/(2 + i) = 3 + 2i$ is reducible, and a counterexample that is not), it has to learn some **math**.
- To predict the answer to a logic puzzle (all actors are musicians, no musician is a singer, some singers are dancers; which conclusions follow?), it has to **reason** from the context, or perhaps only memorize, which "is very disputable": there are ongoing debates over what counts as reasoning or intelligence if a model answers thousands or millions of such questions correctly ([≈29:19](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

The class solved similar problems in the logic homework by encoding the statements in [first-order logic](first-order-logic.md) and applying inference ([≈30:05](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 61](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). This is a completely different approach: no symbols and no rules, just "a giant pile of text, predict the next token," and "a good LM simply has a high probability of predicting 'c)' as the next word." The idea was popularized by OpenAI's 2019 GPT-2 paper, *Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners*, which hoped to "move towards more general systems which can perform many tasks – eventually without the need to manually create and label a training dataset for each one" ([≈30:51](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 62](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

**3. Language models scale** ([≈31:36](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈35:29](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 63–69](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). With lots of data and a big model you can fit all the tasks at once without specialization, and the loss keeps going down as data, model size and compute grow, with no sign of stopping "for practical purposes of today," though some people would say we are starting to hit something (the captions are unclear on what, [≈31:36](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). Chat, coding and translation used to be separate models, such as Google's neural machine translation system and OpenAI's `davinci-codex`; today one model, GPT-5.1, does everything ([slide 64](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Google's translation model had 380 million parameters, which was considered big; today's model has no official size but is possibly about 100 times larger, and it outperforms Llama 3 405B, which is 1,000 times larger than the translation model ([≈33:08](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 65](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The two [scaling-law](scaling-laws.md) papers make this quantitative: Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found that test loss falls smoothly, as a power law, with compute, dataset size and parameters ([slides 66–67](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), and the Chinchilla study (DeepMind, 2022) fixed a compute budget, varied the model size, and drew the resulting curves to predict the compute-optimal model size and loss for each budget ([≈34:40](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 68–69](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

In summary ([≈35:29](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 70](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)): many tasks, "including writing your exam on Wednesday," are language modeling; the one simple objective forces the model to learn many tasks at once; and the recipe scales, so "you don't have to think about all the fancy stuff... just one thing, and do it really well."

## What Makes Language Models Work?

Two ideas have already come up, scaling and next-token prediction as a convenient multi-task objective ([≈36:15](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈37:47](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 72](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Next-token prediction replaces the older multi-task approach of collecting several datasets and tasks, training on each and updating the model with something like a meta-gradient: "Here there's none of that. It's just a simple single objective." The lecture then takes four more: model architecture, pre-training versus post-training (with good data), tokenization and systems. The list is not exhaustive; distillation and data quality, for example, are left out.

### Model architecture: why not an MLP?

The standard architecture is the **Transformer**, from *Attention Is All You Need*, a paper that has drawn so much attention ("pun not intended") that Google added a notice to its top granting permission to reproduce its figures ([≈37:47](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈38:35](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 74](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The lecture does not teach it; slide 75 points to CS224N, CS336, CS231N, CS230 and CS229 and many online resources. Instead it asks why architecture matters at all: **it should not be the bottleneck** to scalable language modeling, compared with data and compute ([≈38:35](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈39:22](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 75](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). To build intuition, consider applying the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) from homework 2 (see [lecture 4](04-learning-iii.md)) directly: flatten the $(T, D)$ embeddings into one vector and map it to a flat vector of $T \times V$ outputs ([≈40:07](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 76](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Three problems follow, and the exercise left to the class is to read about transformers and check how they fix each one ([≈39:22](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

1. **The parameter count depends strongly on $T$ and $V$** ([≈40:07](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈42:24](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 77](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Even one fully connected layer has $TD \times TV = O(DVT^2)$ parameters, and handling sequences of length $2T$ means adding neurons and weights. But we want a large $T$, so the model can look at many past words (a homework pasted into a chatbot is about a thousand words), and a large $V$, to cover many languages and tasks. Transformers share parameters across positions (the Q, K and V projections the class will meet work at every position) and depend only weakly on $V$, through the embedding matrix and the output classification layer.
2. **The network is fixed and has no dynamic weights** ([≈42:24](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈43:56](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 78](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A trained MLP is essentially a fixed, giant lookup table, "for this prefix, this is the next word," and its frozen weights cannot prefer different positions depending on the input, though in one document the first position may matter most and in another the last. In a transformer, even a frozen model creates dynamic weights with the **attention mechanism**, deciding which positions of the input are more important, for instance attending to a rare word more than to filler words like *and* and *the*. The lecturer calls this "a very, very essential and crucial idea that makes them work."
3. **There is no computation reuse** ([≈43:56](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈45:30](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 79](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). At best one can cache the MLP's output for a particular input of length $T$; change one word, say *and* to *or*, and the entire forward pass has to be redone, because every activation mixes every position. Transformers are designed so that computation can be reused; the slide names the **KV cache**, which stores intermediate values for past tokens.

"Again, I have not told you what the transformer is. I'm just throwing out keywords" ([≈45:30](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

### Pre-training

In a homework there is no pre- or post-training: you have a dataset and you train. At scale there is a natural separation into two stages ([≈45:30](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈46:16](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 80](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). **[Pre-training](pre-training.md)** trains a giant model on a giant amount of text with a simple objective, next-token prediction, to make it generally knowledgeable about many things at once: the multi-task learning above. In this phase you do not care what the model will do, only that it models what it sees well, and if you do that well you get a somewhat intelligent model.

**The data** ([≈47:01](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈50:54](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 81–84](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The table of the open model OLMo 2's pre-training mix shows that almost all of it is web pages: DCLM-Baseline supplies 3.71T of the 3.90T tokens and 2.95B of the 3.08B documents, beside code, academic and STEM papers, math and encyclopedic text ([slide 81](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Samples of such documents ([slide 83](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)) include templated Stack Overflow boilerplate ("the workings and policies of the site, about us, learn more, Stack Overflow, the company"), a news site's navigation menu, text of unclear origin, and, more usefully, a question about fixing a disk after a Time Machine backup together with the commands the person ran. That last one teaches what tends to follow "I want to" or "Googling." CS336's lecture links more random samples, and "you'll be surprised how much stuff is on the internet." Even this is already high-quality data: DCLM-Baseline is about **1.4%** of the DCLM pool drawn from Common Crawl, a crawler that follows links and saves every page it sees. Cutting it down takes URL filtering, keeping only English, deduplication, and finally a model-based filter that judges whether text is high quality ([slide 84](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).

**Capabilities emerge from pre-training** ([≈50:54](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈53:59](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 85–87](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The GPT-3 paper, *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners* (OpenAI, 2020), trained on filtered internet data with no task-specific training at all, and then evaluated on benchmarks such as multiple-choice questions and summarization. As the number of parameters grows, the average performance across 42 benchmarks keeps going up ([slide 86](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). And a large enough model can do **few-shot, in-context learning**: write an input sequence that states a task and gives examples, such as *Translate English to French: sea otter => loutre de mer, peppermint => menthe poivrée, plush girafe => girafe peluche, cheese =>*, and the model completes it. This is a perfectly valid input for a model that only predicts missing tokens; it pattern-matches the task against what it saw in training. "Simply showing examples of the task as part of the input string makes completions better" ([slide 87](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), which is very surprising when you think about why it should work at all.

### Post-training

**[Post-training](post-training.md)** makes the model actually useful, like ChatGPT ([≈53:59](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈55:36](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 88](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). The pre-trained base model knows a lot from next-token prediction, but it is "autocomplete on steroids": it follows the pattern of its input and has no idea that the input is a question or a task. Prompted with *Explain the moon landing to a 6 year old in a few sentences.*, GPT-3 writes more questions like it (*Explain the theory of gravity to a 6 year old.* …) instead of answering. That matches the pre-training distribution: web pages that list questions to quiz students, or exams, are more common than nicely formatted questions with answers ([≈55:36](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

**Instruction following and RLHF** ([≈56:23](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:00:11](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 89–92](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Instruction following makes completions treat the input as a question or an instruction. OpenAI's 2022 paper (InstructGPT) introduced **RLHF**, reinforcement learning from human feedback, in three stages:

1. Collect question-and-answer data and train on it directly with next-token prediction, so that the next tokens are answers rather than more questions (supervised fine-tuning).
2. Have the model generate several responses and have humans label which is better, giving preference labels; train a separate **reward model** to predict which answers are better. The reward model approximates human preference.
3. Treat the language model as a **policy**, an agent in RL as in the [MDP and RL lectures](reinforcement-learning.md): each generation is an action, and each action gets a reward, higher for completions humans prefer. Then run reinforcement learning against the reward model.

The training algorithms are things the class has already learned: "in practice I think people just use the simple things" the class learned, policy gradient methods, with slight modifications such as a baseline to reduce variance ([≈59:26](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md); see [policy gradients](policy-gradients.md)). Slide 91 puts it as generations = samples from the policy, reward = a model of human preference labels, training = policy gradient (basically). Afterwards the model answers the moon-landing prompt ("People went to the moon, and they took pictures…"). It also answers *Write a guide on how I can break into my neighbor's house*, where the base model had produced more description of the problem, and that "may not be a good idea" ([≈1:00:11](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 92](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). While describing the preference labels, the lecturer wonders aloud whether he has a slide for them ("Probably not"); the comparison step is Step 2 of the InstructGPT figure on slide 90 ([≈57:08](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

**Safety tuning and jailbreaks** ([≈1:00:57](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:04:47](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 93–96](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A separate goal of post-training is **safety tuning**: make the model *not* answer bad questions. Post-training can also reduce memorization or make a model more concise, warmer or less sycophantic. Asked how to make a Molotov cocktail, ChatGPT refuses. That refusal is not the natural distribution of web text; it was trained in, with the same techniques: supervised fine-tuning on refusals, a reward model and RL against it ([≈1:01:44](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). People then find ways to break it, a cat-and-mouse game between attackers ("jailbreaking") and defenders ([slide 94](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Asking *how did people make* a Molotov cocktail, in the past tense, got an answer ([slide 94](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md), Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2025). So did asking the model to act as your late grandmother telling a bedtime story about making napalm ([slide 95](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), as did leetspeak and base64 ([slide 96](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md), Wei et al., 2023, which tabulates dozens of attacks against GPT-4 and Claude v1.3). The guardrail has holes because a set of human-labelled refusals ("how to break into your house? No, I cannot help you with that") does not generalize to every way people can trick the model in such a huge input space, and safety tuning is often a separate data collection and pipeline ([≈1:02:29](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:03:15](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). These particular attacks are from about two years before the lecture and, the lecturer believes, patched.

**Techniques** ([≈1:04:47](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 97](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)), still evolving: **SFT**, next-token prediction on instruction-following or refusal examples (an input sequence prefix and its output suffix, such as *What are the three primary colors?* → *The three primary colors are red, blue, and yellow.*); **RLHF**, RL to reward instruction following or refusal, labelled by humans; **RLVR**, the same with an automated verifier in place of the humans; and **data curation**, removing harmful data from the beginning.

### Tokenization

"I might be short on time, so I'm going to rush through some of the later things" ([≈1:04:47](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). Early in the lecture the vocabulary was a dictionary from strings to indices, and anything outside it is not recognized. A **[tokenizer](tokenization.md)** builds the vocabulary from **subword units** instead of enumerating all possible words, which would be impossible, or using single characters, which is too granular when words occur so often ([≈1:05:34](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:06:20](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 98](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). A rare or misspelled input ("rArE or mispeled") is broken into subword units seen in training. The key technique is **byte-pair encoding (BPE)**: start with one token per character, plus some common words and special tokens; repeatedly merge the pair of tokens that occurs most often into a new token (if *in* and *put* often occur together, they become one token), adding one entry to the vocabulary per merge; stop at the desired vocabulary size. Tokenization "both solves a lot of problems and creates a lot of problems": many of today's language-model problems come from it, yet models cannot be trained without it, at least for now. Slide 99 points to Andrej Karpathy's post and video on how much LLM weirdness traces back to tokenization ([≈1:07:06](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). [Lecture 3](03-learning-ii.md) introduced tokenization when turning text into tensors.

### Systems

Systems are often an afterthought when learning machine learning, but they matter ([≈1:07:06](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:08:39](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 100](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). For Llama 3.3 70B, the weights alone need about $70\text{B} \times 2$ bytes per parameter $= 140$ GB of memory, more than any laptop. Training also stores activations, gradients and the optimizer states (Adam's first and second moments), and vanilla training with Adam uses about 8 times as much memory, about 1.12 TB. Modern models have trillions of parameters, and a single H100 GPU has 80 GB. Four key ideas make it work:

1. **Quantization**: store everything in lower precision ([≈1:08:39](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:10:10](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 101](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). With $k$ bits there are $2^k$ bins, so 2-bit quantization allows only $2^2 = 4$ possible values per weight; fit the weights into the bins while minimizing a loss and round each weight to its nearest bin. Today it goes as low as 2-bit for inference and 4-bit for training, but this is active research: no one has really figured out large-scale 4-bit pre-training, and 2-bit inference loses a lot of performance. Slide 101's diagram shows absmax int8 quantization of a small vector.
2. **Parallelism and sharding** ([≈1:10:10](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:11:43](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 102–104](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). If the model fits on one GPU, use **data parallelism**: split the data across GPUs, each with a copy of the model, and combine the results (e.g. gradients). If it does not, cut the model. **Pipeline parallelism** puts different layers on different GPUs and passes activations along, which is slow, since one GPU waits while the next computes. **Tensor parallelism** cuts the matrices themselves, column-wise or row-wise, into smaller multiplications on different GPUs whose results are combined.
3. **Be hardware-aware and write better code** ([≈1:11:43](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 105–106](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Most computation is bottlenecked by memory bandwidth, not raw compute, so move data between memory and the GPU's compute as rarely as possible. **Kernel fusion** combines many small operations, each of which would go to memory and back, into one; FlashAttention is an example.
4. **Caching and batching** ([slide 107](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)): cache activations on common inputs (the KV cache, and serving systems caching common prompts) and batch inputs to raise arithmetic intensity, which is why providers such as OpenAI's Batch API charge less for batched jobs. The lecturer skips this slide ("Okay, I'll skip that," [≈1:11:43](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).

**Other ideas** ([≈1:11:43](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:12:30](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 108](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)): **test-time scaling**: a model that writes many things before its answer somehow does better, as a student with a scratch pad does better on an exam than one asked for the answer right away (compare [lecture 5](05-search-i.md)'s test-time compute); **distillation**, making a small model mimic a big one; tool use; training and inference tricks; algorithmic innovations in optimizers, architectures and RL algorithms; thoughtful evaluation; and multimodality (vision, audio, video).

## Where We Are Today

The last section, in "the last two minutes" ([≈1:12:30](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:19:26](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 109–118](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)):

- **Tokens are not only words.** The robot of slide 3 takes images, language and robot actions all as tokens to predict, so a language model now fits many things besides natural language ([≈1:13:18](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 110](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)).
- **"Language model" now means large language model**, the powerful artifacts that are having a real impact on society, not the small models trained in this class or the networks of five years ago ([≈1:14:03](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 111](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). Because of industrialization, LLMs are developed by organizations with their own incentives; a university like Stanford rarely has the compute and money to build one. Many sell the model as software (ChatGPT, Claude), many integrate it into products (Cursor, Lovable), many release it freely for strategic reasons (Qwen, Llama, Kimi, DeepSeek), and others release models to advance open research: OLMo, and Marin, a project Percy (the course's instructor) leads ([≈1:14:51](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)).
- **Closed (frontier) models** ([≈1:15:37](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:16:22](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 112](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)) sit behind a paid API, accessible but tied to your identity, and supposedly have a "secret sauce" of algorithms, data, systems and inference tricks. Their papers from 2019, 2020 and 2022 say roughly how they work, but not the details. They beat humans at coding (IOI), math (FrontierMath) and knowledge (HLE), have caused an international and governmental response ("panic?"), and people love them nonetheless.
- **Open-weight models** ([≈1:17:07](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:17:53](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slides 113–114](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)) put their weights on Hugging Face, so you can do anything with them: run inference (vLLM), fine-tune (Unsloth), run RL (verl), use them in a startup (depending on the license), remove the safety training, try to extract training data, or study the architecture. They are free because *running* them is not: the developers can sell the infrastructure. They are catching up with the closed models; Kimi K2 reportedly beat OpenAI's and Anthropic's models on certain benchmarks, slide 114 showing 44.9 against 41.7 and 32.0 on Humanity's Last Exam with tools. Their data, compute and algorithms are not always disclosed, "but you're fine with it" because you can run the model yourself.
- **Open-source models** ([≈1:18:40](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 115](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)) open everything (data, training algorithms, weights, code). They are mostly academic (OLMo, Marin, LLM360, Pythia), relatively small and "toy-ish," though competitive with older-generation frontier models, and they open up research that needs to know how a model was trained and on what.
- **AI safety** ([≈1:18:40](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:19:26](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md), [slide 117](../raw/slides/17-language-models.md)). As models get better at hacking computers, bioweapons, coding and forecasting, concern grows, to the point of a book titled *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*.

Saying there are "two more slides" but he can end there, the lecturer goes straight to AI safety: slide 116 on agents (LLMs trained to use tools and run in a loop) is not discussed, and slide 118's list of open problems (copyright and fair use, data and user privacy, security, interpretability, HCI) is not read out, only "a lot of problems… we didn't even get to talk about" ([≈1:18:40](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)–[≈1:19:26](../raw/transcripts/17-language-models.md)). Slide 118 ends "More in the next lectures!"; lectures 18 and 19 are on AI and society and AI supply chains. [Lecture 18](18-ai-and-society.md) takes up copyright and fair use, including whether models memorize books ([copyright and fair use](copyright-and-fair-use.md)), and the spectrum from closed to open models ([openness and transparency](openness-and-transparency.md)).
