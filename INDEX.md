# CS221 — Artificial Intelligence: Principles and Techniques | Autumn 2025 — Percy Liang

Stanford's introductory AI course. Covers perception, reasoning, action, and learning under resource constraints, drawing on symbolic, neural, and statistical AI traditions. This knowledge base covers all 20 lectures, with edited transcripts, slide transcriptions, and interlinked wiki pages. Lecture 20, the closing fireside chat, has no slides or other course material, so its page is built from the transcript alone and reports the instructor's personal views.

## Lectures

- **[Lecture 1 — Course Overview and AI Foundations](wiki/01-course-overview.md)** — What is AI? The four ingredients of intelligence (perceive, reason, act, learn), resource constraints, alignment and societal goals, history of AI through three traditions (symbolic, neural, statistical), and an introduction to tensors and einops.
- **[Lecture 2 — Learning I](wiki/02-learning-i.md)** — Einops review, gradients and partial derivatives, computation graphs and backpropagation (building a mini-PyTorch), and linear regression as the first complete ML pipeline: hypothesis class → loss function → optimization algorithm.
- **[Lecture 3 — Learning II](wiki/03-learning-ii.md)** — Linear classification: from zero-one loss (zero gradients → optimization fails) through logistic regression (maximum likelihood with a smooth, differentiable loss), logistic function and its properties, multiclass extension via softmax and cross entropy, and representing text as tensors (tokenization → one-hot → bag of words).
- **[Lecture 4 — Learning III](wiki/04-learning-iii.md)** — Deep neural networks: PyTorch as NumPy + autodiff, the feature-map insight (linear in φ-space = nonlinear in x-space), MLPs with ReLU activation (why two linear layers don't help but one ReLU does), deep networks and the vanishing/exploding gradient problem, and five survival techniques: residual connections, layer normalization, Xavier initialization, activation function choice, and SGD/Adam optimizers.
- **[Lecture 5 — Search I](wiki/05-search-i.md)** — Search as reasoning: the search-problem abstraction (start_state, successors, is_end), the travel problem and what belongs in the state, future cost and exhaustive search (exponential time), dynamic programming = exhaustive search + caching, approximate search via best-of-n and beam search, and test-time compute in language models cast as a search problem (the Bitter Lesson's "search and learning").
- **[Lecture 6 — Search II](wiki/06-search-ii.md)** — Search with cycles: uniform cost search (past costs in increasing order, priority queue, correctness proof) and A* (UCS with heuristic-modified costs, consistency and admissibility, telescoping-sums correctness). Then the relaxation recipe for heuristics: knock down walls (Manhattan distance), free tram (fewer states), overlapping tiles (independent subproblems), and combining heuristics with max.
- **[Lecture 7 — Markov Decision Processes](wiki/07-markov-decision-processes.md)** — Search with stochastic actions: the MDP formalism (states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, discount), policies as solutions, rollouts and expected utility, Monte Carlo estimation, and the two exact algorithms — policy evaluation (value of a given policy) and value iteration (value and construction of the optimal policy), differing by a single max. Running examples: the flaky tram (walk until 5, then tram) and the dice game (stay: 12 > 10).
- **[Lecture 8 — Reinforcement Learning](wiki/08-reinforcement-learning.md)** — MDPs without the model: the agent/environment loop, get_action and incorporate_feedback, and four algorithms on the flaky tram — model-based value iteration (estimate the MDP, then value-iterate: explore −9, exploit −6.6), model-free Monte Carlo (average rollout utilities into Q-values), SARSA (bootstrap mid-rollout, on-policy), and Q-learning (bootstrap off-policy to estimate Q*). The leaderboard and the epsilon-greedy exploration/exploitation balance.
- **[Lecture 9 — Policy Gradient](wiki/09-policy-gradient.md)** — RL beyond lookup tables: function approximation (parameterized Q_θ(s, a) with features φ(s, a) for image/sentence states), then learning the policy directly as a probabilistic classifier π_θ(a | s). The policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE (imitation learning on your own demonstrations, weighted by utility), and variance reduction — baselines, returns-to-go, actor-critic.
- **[Lecture 10 — Games I](wiki/10-games-i.md)** — Games complete the tour of state-based models: the game tree (two-player, zero-sum), game evaluation via the V_eval recurrence, then expectimax (optimal against a known opponent) and minimax (assume the opponent plays optimally — the face-off shows each "optimal" policy is only optimal against its baked-in assumption), expectiminimax with chance nodes, and the two speedups: alpha-beta pruning (exact) and evaluation functions (approximate, depth-limited).
- **[Lecture 11 — Games II](wiki/11-games-ii.md)** — Two halves. TD learning learns a game's evaluation function: estimate V_π(s) by bootstrapping (TD : V_π :: SARSA : Q_π), why state values suffice when the rules are known, why RL anyway (exponential states), self-play with argmax/argmin over one value function, backgammon features, and Samuel's checkers → TD-Gammon → AlphaGo Zero. Then simultaneous games (two-finger Morra, payoff matrices, pure vs. mixed strategies, going second is no worse, the 7/12 strategy and −1/12 value, von Neumann's minimax theorem) and non-zero-sum games (prisoner's dilemma, Nash equilibrium and Nash's existence theorem).
- **[Lecture 12 — Bayesian Networks I](wiki/12-bayesian-networks-i.md)** — The third unit opens: representing the world as random variables. Model-free vs. model-based recap; probability as tensors (joint distribution, marginalization, conditioning as select–sum–divide, all in einsum); probabilistic inference as SQL queries on the joint; the four-step construction (variables, DAG, local conditionals, product) on the alarm network (P(B=1 | A=1) ≈ 0.51, dropping to 0.05 given the earthquake) and medical diagnosis (itchy eyes lower P(cold | cough) from 0.28 to 0.13); explaining away; lowercase p vs. uppercase P; language models as Bayesian networks and inference as jailbreaking; probabilistic programs, rejection sampling and a hidden Markov model; Bayesian networks vs. classifiers.
- **[Lecture 13 — Bayesian Networks and Gibbs Sampling](wiki/13-gibbs-sampling.md)** — Faster approximate inference, then structure. Review (joint distribution as a database, exact inference by slice–marginalize–sum–divide, 2^100 assignments; rejection sampling, 0.44 from 300 samples). Gibbs sampling: start from an assignment that satisfies the evidence, resample one variable at a time from its conditional given all others (MCMC; no rejection, but correlated samples); the telephone network A → B → C worked by hand (0.02, 0.32 → B pulled to 1) and in code (0.68 vs. rejection's 0.65); running time; the Markov blanket (parents, children and, as asked in class, co-parents) as a speedup; Gibbs on the alarm (0.65, then 0.335, vs. 0.51); when each sampler fails (rare evidence vs. highly correlated variables, the three-legged race); Metropolis–Hastings and mixing times. Then independence and conditional independence on four small networks (A → C ← B independent until C is observed; A ← C → B the reverse), the alarm, the shade–prune–marry–path procedure, and the medical-diagnosis examples; one printed line the course later deleted.
- **[Lecture 14 — Bayesian Networks and Learning](wiki/14-bayesian-networks-learning.md)** — Where the probabilities come from. Review of inference and conditional independence restated as blocked paths (chain, fork, v-structure; the whiteboard game). Learning from fully observed data by count and normalize, built up on movie ratings: one variable, genre → rating, a v-structure (condition on all parents together), an inverted v-structure, parameter sharing (one table powering two users' ratings; reading vs. writing), the hidden Markov model's start/trans/emit tables, and a general 15-line learner. Why it works: count and normalize is the closed-form maximum likelihood estimate. Laplace smoothing (pseudocounts; λ = 1 gives 2/7 and 1/7; λ → 0, λ → ∞, more data washes it out). Expectation maximization with the genre hidden: E-step weights, M-step weighted counts, five traced iterations from 0.69 to 1.0, local optima, symmetry breaking, label permutation.
- **[Lecture 15 — Logic I](wiki/15-logic-i.md)** — The logic unit opens: logical reasoning as algebra rather than search ($A + B = 10$, $A - B = 4$), why logic (expressivity, versus its old problems with uncertainty and data), and a logic as a language with three ingredients: syntax, semantics, inference rules. Propositional logic worked out in full: recursive syntax; models and the interpretation function (the $(\neg A \wedge B) \leftrightarrow C$ evaluation tree); the models of a formula and of a knowledge base; entailment, contradiction and contingency, and the Ask (yes / no / I don't know) and Tell (I already knew that / I don't buy that / I learned something new) operations; the mapping to Bayesian networks and $P(f \mid \text{KB})$; satisfiability, the two-call reduction and model checking with Z3; modus ponens and forward inference; soundness and completeness (the water glass), with modus ponens sound and the reverse rule unsound.
- **[Lecture 16 — Logic II](wiki/16-logic-ii.md)** — First-order logic. A knowledge base about Alice, Phoenix and the weather answers "Is it snowing?" with I don't know, I don't know, then No; a review of lecture 15; why propositional logic is clunky (all students know arithmetic; even integers as sums of two primes). Syntax: terms (constants, variables, functions) denote objects, predicates applied to terms give atomic formulas, quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$, and what is not a formula. Semantics: why assigning truth values to atomic formulas fails, a model as a domain plus an interpretation (the $o_1, o_2, o_3$ graph), and the recursive interpretation function evaluated on Knows(alice, arithmetic) and $\forall x$. Propositionalization under unique names and domain closure. Modus ponens on definite clauses with substitution and unification (deriving Knows(alice, logic)), its complexity, sound but not complete (resolution skipped). Translating English into first-order logic ($\forall$ with $\rightarrow$, $\exists$ with $\wedge$), and higher-order logics.
- **[Lecture 17 — Introduction to Language Models](wiki/17-language-models.md)** — A slide-deck lecture rather than a program, given by Ken Liu (not on the exam). The scale of Qwen3 and Llama 3 (36T tokens, the 6ND rule's 3.9 × 10^25 FLOPs, 880,000 H100-days, \$42M). What a language model is: language as vocabulary plus grammar, "The stock market crashed and investors ___", the tensor view (T, D, V, B shapes; predicting at each position; greedy decoding vs. sampling; autoregression; batching), the probabilistic view (chain rule), next-token prediction vs. masked LM (BERT), and three implementations (count strings, n-grams with the Markov assumption, P(crashed | stock, market) = 1/2, neural networks trained on the input shifted by one). Why model language: many tasks are language modeling, multi-task learning (Turing, irreducible fractions, a logic puzzle; GPT-2), scaling (Kaplan, Chinchilla). What makes it work: why not an MLP (O(DVT²) parameters, no dynamic weights, no computation reuse vs. attention and the KV cache), pre-training (OLMo 2's data, DCLM's 1.4% of Common Crawl, GPT-3's in-context learning), post-training (RLHF as policy gradient, safety tuning, past-tense and grandma jailbreaks), byte-pair encoding, systems (140 GB of weights, quantization, data/pipeline/tensor parallelism, kernel fusion). Where we are: closed, open-weight and open-source models, AI safety.
- **[Lecture 18 — AI & Society](wiki/18-ai-and-society.md)** — The course turns from technique to societal impact. Why technologists should care (ChatGPT's 800 million weekly users, design choices only builders make, Tom Lehrer's Wernher von Braun); the Belmont Report and ACM Code of Ethics, and the problem of operationalizing them; AI as a dual use technology; the intent × impact grid of beneficial applications, misuse and accidents (inequality, sycophancy, overreliance, cultural homogenization, jobs); the ecosystem view (upstream data, labor and compute; downstream users). Deep dives: Gender Shades and third-party auditing, distributionally robust optimization, a reward model that favors Western countries, the chest-drain spurious correlation (AUC 0.94 vs. 0.77); alignment through the RL recipe (CoastRunners reward hacking, overoptimization, pluralism, scalable oversight); copyright law, licenses, the four fair-use factors, terms of service, and memorization versus extraction (Llama 3.1 70B and *Harry Potter*); the Foundation Model Transparency Index (every cell of the May 2024 heat map) and the spectrum from closed to open-development models, with marginal risk.
- **[Lecture 19 — Economics of AI (AI Supply Chains)](wiki/19-economics-of-ai.md)** — A guest lecture by Rishi Bommasani, given from a 70-page Google Slides deck. Why the economics of AI matters (the Mag 7 at 35% of the S&P 500, junior software-developer hiring after ChatGPT, a call-center AI tool helping the newest workers most). The dual lens of technology and organizations: Google, Anthropic and OpenAI score alike on a benchmark but differ in release, pricing, products and partners. Supply chains: compute (the semiconductor value chain; ASML, TSMC and Nvidia; resilience, value and US–China geopolitics; the clouds; data centers' land, power and water), data (six acquisition methods and how each is priced, robots.txt restrictions from Longpre et al., the Anthropic settlement as \$3,000 a work, running out of data, compliance, competition) and distribution (a seven-level access table from fully closed to fully open; vertical control, pricing, data flows, adaptability). Growth: micro vs. macro, Nate Silver's technological Richter scale and forecasts from modest to utopia and doom; general-purpose technologies (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg) and why AI qualifies (pervasiveness, improvement over time, complementary innovations); the productivity J-curve; GDP's 2% trend, the Solow paradox and GDP-B (\$97B of consumer surplus); and what GDP does if AI makes one sector productive (Baumol's cost disease), injects cheap labor ($Y = A \cdot K^{\alpha} \cdot L^{1-\alpha}$) or generates ideas (Romer). Ends with three student questions.
- **[Lecture 20 — Fireside Chat, Conclusion](wiki/20-fireside-chat-conclusion.md)** — The last session: head CA Ken Liu interviews Percy Liang with submitted and live questions (personal views, not exam material; no slides). His path into AI (MIT, a 2005 hidden-Markov-model language model whose word clusters were his "emergent capabilities" moment). AI outlook: AI escaping the lab, science-fiction baggage vs. AI as hidden infrastructure, next-token prediction and long-context loss as underhyped and thinking traces as overhyped, academia's role (blue-sky research; memorization and evaluation work industry is not incentivized to do), entry-level software jobs and the shift from doing to deciding what to do, foundation models for science data, AI as the technology of the century, and why the Turing test should give way to counting scientific discoveries. The class: this year's redesign (societal impact, executable lectures from CS336), how search and MDPs connect to test-time compute and RL post-training, CS221 vs. CS229, CS224N and CS336, research as taking bets (decentralized training), becoming a CA. Career advice: prioritize growth and exploration, good people, circuitous paths, learning to adapt, grit and collaboration; the AI bubble; three reasons AI companies are not transparent; getting users to care about ethics; piano and music foundation models; how to get into research at Stanford.

## Topic Pages

- **[Tensors](wiki/tensors.md)** — Multi-dimensional arrays, the atoms of modern ML. Tensor order, shapes in different domains (vision, language, tabular), creating and viewing tensors, matrix multiplication and broadcasting, and why tensor ops are efficient.
- **[Einops](wiki/einops.md)** — Named-axis tensor operations for readable, self-documenting code. `einsum` for generalized matrix multiplication, `reduce` for axis aggregation, and `rearrange` for splitting/merging dimensions (essential for multi-head attention). Lecture 12 reuses `einsum` for probability: marginalization, conditioning and Bayesian-network joints.
- **[The Turing Test](wiki/turing-test.md)** — Lecture 1's account of Turing's 1950 imitation game and why it mattered (grounding "can machines think?" in objective measurement, with the solution left open), and lecture 20's argument that it is no longer a good measure (imitation, humans as the bar, static tests are gameable) and should give way to counting new scientific discoveries.
- **[Backpropagation](wiki/backpropagation.md)** — Reverse-mode automatic differentiation. Computation graphs, the chain rule, forward and backward passes, node-level gradient rules (Add, Multiply, Squared), and how PyTorch works under the hood.
- **[Linear Regression](wiki/linear-regression.md)** — The simplest complete ML pipeline. Hypothesis class (parameterized linear functions), squared loss, and training loss minimization via gradient descent. Demonstrates the general ML template.
- **[Gradient Descent](wiki/gradient-descent.md)** — Iterative optimization by moving in the negative gradient direction. Learning rate tradeoffs, convex vs. non-convex convergence, and variants (SGD, Adam).
- **[Search Problems](wiki/search-problems.md)** — The abstraction that turns word problems into code: state, successors (action–cost–next-state), is_end, and the minimum-total-cost objective. What belongs in the state, why keeping it small matters, and the Bitter Lesson motivation.
- **[Dynamic Programming](wiki/dynamic-programming.md)** — Exhaustive search plus caching: Bellman's terminology, the future-cost recurrence, the three-line diff from exhaustive search, and when the cache pays off (states fit in memory, many paths to a state).
- **[Beam Search and Best-of-n](wiki/beam-search.md)** — The two approximate search methods: n random rollouts of a policy, and keeping the best `beam_width` partial solutions. The width-1/infinity extremes, determinism vs. stochasticity, the policy-as-prior distinction, and particle filtering.
- **[Uniform Cost Search](wiki/uniform-cost-search.md)** — Dijkstra's algorithm for cyclic search problems: past cost vs. future cost, the explored/frontier/unexplored buckets, priority-queue mechanics (in-place updates, stale entries), the correctness proof, and why non-negative costs are required.
- **[A* Search](wiki/a-star-search.md)** — UCS with heuristic-shifted costs: ordering by PastCost + h(s), the modified-cost trick, consistency (and admissibility), correctness via telescoping sums, the efficiency bound, and the counterexample of a bad heuristic.
- **[Heuristics from Relaxation](wiki/heuristics.md)** — How to construct heuristics that are consistent by construction: solve a relaxed problem (fewer costs) and use its future cost. The three structured relaxations (closed form, fewer states, independent subproblems), fair accounting, and max-combining heuristics.
- **[Markov Decision Processes](wiki/markov-decision-processes.md)** — Search problems with stochastic actions: the formal definition (T, R, γ), why the solution must be a policy, rollouts and discounted utility, and the two running examples (flaky tram, dice game).
- **[Monte Carlo Policy Evaluation](wiki/monte-carlo-evaluation.md)** — Estimating V_π by averaging rollout utilities: the 1/√n convergence rate, the coin-flip intuition, and the lecture's numbers (−11.7 vs the true −12).
- **[Policy Evaluation](wiki/policy-evaluation.md)** — Computing V_π exactly: Q-values as the recurrence's building block, bootstrapping, the L∞ convergence test, the two phases of convergence, and the tram/dice demos (27 iterations → −12; 2/3-decay → 12).
- **[Value Iteration](wiki/value-iteration.md)** — Computing V* and the optimal policy: the max-over-actions recurrence (one line from policy evaluation), Bellman's 1957 foundation, the flaky-tram optimum (walk until 5, then tram; −7.33), and why the optimal policy is deterministic for MDPs.
- **[Reinforcement Learning](wiki/reinforcement-learning.md)** — MDPs where the agent doesn't know T or R: the agent interface (get_action / incorporate_feedback), policy vs. agent, the four-algorithm map (model-based vs. model-free, MC vs. bootstrapping, on- vs. off-policy), epsilon-greedy exploration, and the world-models debate.
- **[Q-Learning](wiki/q-learning.md)** — Estimating Q-values without the MDP, in three steps: Monte Carlo averaging (wait for the episode), SARSA (bootstrap mid-rollout, on-policy), and Q-learning (bootstrap off-policy to target Q*). The gradient update, the one-line code difference, and policy improvement.
- **[Policy Gradients](wiki/policy-gradients.md)** — The third RL family: parameterize the policy π_θ(a | s) directly and gradient-ascent expected utility. The policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE, and the variance-reduction toolkit — baselines (mean-zero, unbiased), returns-to-go, and actor-critic bootstrapping.
- **[Games](wiki/games.md)** — State-based models with an adversary: the game formalism (five methods, sparse utility, per-state players), the value of a game (V_eval recurrence and Monte Carlo), expectimax against a known opponent, expectiminimax with chance nodes, and the face-off — the matrix (1, 2, −5, 5) showing every "optimal" policy is only optimal against its assumption.
- **[Minimax](wiki/minimax.md)** — Assume the opponent plays optimally: the max/min recurrence, the halving-game solution (winning n: 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11), perfect play and solved games (strongly solved tic-tac-toe vs. unsolved chess/Go), and why minimax is a lower bound against any opponent — and beatable if you know yours.
- **[Alpha-Beta Pruning](wiki/alpha-beta-pruning.md)** — Exact minimax speedup via branch and bound: α lower bounds on max nodes, β upper bounds on min nodes, prune when bounds don't overlap every ancestor. The worked 9, 7 / 6, [3, 4] [7, 9] / 8, 3 example, and why ordering determines how much you prune.
- **[Evaluation Functions](wiki/evaluation-functions.md)** — Approximate speedup: search to depth d, then guess with prior knowledge. The Deep-Blue-style chess evaluation (material 9/5/3/1, mobility 0.1), depth-limited minimax, and the bridge to Q-values — evaluation functions can be learned (lecture 11's TD learning).
- **[TD Learning](wiki/td-learning.md)** — Estimating state values V_π(s; w) from experience by bootstrapping: the prediction/target update with the target held constant, why V suffices in games (known deterministic rules) and why RL is still needed (exponential states), on-policy and bootstrapping, self-play (agent argmax, opponent argmin, dice as a fixed player), and the checkers / TD-Gammon / AlphaGo Zero history.
- **[Simultaneous Games](wiki/simultaneous-games.md)** — Zero-sum games where both players move at once: payoff matrices, pure and mixed strategies, game evaluation, going second is no worse (max-min ≤ min-max), the second player can play pure, solving two-finger Morra (p = 7/12, value −1/12), and von Neumann's minimax theorem — revealing an optimal mixed strategy doesn't hurt.
- **[Nash Equilibrium](wiki/nash-equilibrium.md)** — Non-zero-sum games: competitive vs. cooperative utilities, the prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix, the Nash equilibrium definition and Nash's 1950 existence theorem, the three examples (Morra, collaborative Morra's two equilibria, both-testify), and stability vs. optimality.
- **[Bayesian Networks](wiki/bayesian-networks.md)** — Defining a joint distribution node by node: the four steps (random variables, DAG, one local conditional distribution per node given all its parents, product), edges as direct influence rather than causation, p (defined) vs. P (derived), the alarm, medical-diagnosis, language-model and hidden-Markov-model networks, probabilistic programs, and the advantages over classifiers (missing data, prior knowledge, interpretability, causality).
- **[Probabilistic Inference](wiki/probabilistic-inference.md)** — Asking a joint distribution P(query | evidence): marginalization and conditioning (select, compute the probability of the evidence, divide), unmentioned variables summed out, the SQL-database analogy, inference as einsum with indicator tensors, a table of the lecture's worked answers, and why exact inference gets expensive.
- **[Explaining Away](wiki/explaining-away.md)** — Two independent causes of an observed effect become dependent: the burglary/earthquake alarm (0.05 → 0.51 → 0.05) and the medical-diagnosis case where itchy eyes, not a cause of the cough, still halve the probability of a cold via allergies.
- **[Rejection Sampling](wiki/rejection-sampling.md)** — Approximate inference: write the network as a probabilistic program, draw samples, keep those matching the evidence, histogram the query. The five-line algorithm, the lecture's estimates against exact answers (10 vs. 1,000 samples), the hidden Markov model tracking example, convergence, and why rare evidence makes it slow. Lecture 13's review runs and its comparison with Gibbs sampling (complementary failure cases).
- **[Gibbs Sampling](wiki/gibbs-sampling.md)** — MCMC inference in Bayesian networks: keep an assignment that satisfies the evidence and resample one variable at a time from its conditional given the rest. The update as a ratio of joint probabilities (no joint table built), the `gibbs_sampling` code, the board picture of the chain, running time, a table of the lecture's estimates against exact answers, why it beats rejection sampling on rare evidence and gets stuck on highly correlated variables, and Metropolis–Hastings and mixing times.
- **[Markov Blanket](wiki/markov-blanket.md)** — The variables a Gibbs update needs: why factors not involving the sampled variable cancel, the lecture's definition (parents and children) and the co-parents answer from class, the telephone network's blankets, and the running-time saving.
- **[Conditional Independence](wiki/conditional-independence.md)** — Reading independence off a Bayesian network's graph: definitions, the four small networks (no edge, A → B, A → C ← B, A ← C → B) marginally and given C, the alarm and explaining away, the four-step shade–prune–marry–path procedure, the medical-diagnosis examples, and lecture 14's restatement as a blocked-paths rule.
- **[Maximum Likelihood](wiki/maximum-likelihood.md)** — Choose the parameters that make the data most probable: the product/sum-of-logs objective, the link to cross entropy and language-model training, lecture 3's logistic loss, and lecture 14's result that count and normalize is the closed-form maximum likelihood estimate for a Bayesian network (the Lagrange-multiplier sketch for one variable, the regrouping argument for two).
- **[Parameter Sharing](wiki/parameter-sharing.md)** — One local conditional distribution powering several nodes: the two-users example with and without sharing, the "tables floating out" picture, counts belonging to parameters rather than variables, reading vs. writing, the fewer-parameters/more-flexibility trade-off, and the general form with table types $d_i$.
- **[Hidden Markov Models](wiki/hidden-markov-models.md)** — Hidden states in a chain, each emitting a noisy observation: the structure and joint distribution, the three shared tables (start, trans, emit) whatever the length, lecture 12's object-tracking program queried by rejection sampling, and lecture 14's learning by counting on two sequences.
- **[Laplace Smoothing](wiki/laplace-smoothing.md)** — Pseudocounts against maximum likelihood's zeros: add λ to every count, then normalize. The ratings-1-and-4 example (2/7, 1/7), the traced table for λ = 0, 1 and 20 and for 1,000 more examples, and the lecturer's advice (1 or 0.1, not 20).
- **[Expectation Maximization](wiki/expectation-maximization.md)** — Learning with a hidden variable: maximize the likelihood of the observed data, the chicken-and-egg loop of E-step (weighted completions) and M-step (weighted count and normalize), the genre example iterated from 0.69 to 1.0, a messier data set, clustering, and the caveats (local optima, non-uniform initialization, label permutation).
- **[Propositional Logic](wiki/propositional-logic.md)** — The simplest logic, and the vehicle for general concepts: logic as a language (syntax, semantics, inference rules), why not natural language, the recursive syntax of symbols and connectives, models as assignments, the recursive interpretation function, the set of models $\mathcal{M}(f)$ and compactness, knowledge bases as intersections or conjunctions, and why adding facts only shrinks the possible worlds.
- **[Entailment, Contradiction and Contingency](wiki/entailment.md)** — How far adding a formula shrinks a knowledge base's models: not at all (entailment), to nothing (contradiction), or in between (contingency), with the lecture's Rain/Wet examples; contradiction of $f$ as entailment of $\neg f$; Ask and Tell and their three answers; what Tell does not guarantee (fiction, who is at fault, monotonicity); and the probabilistic generalization $P(f \mid \text{KB})$.
- **[Satisfiability](wiki/satisfiability.md)** — Whether any world is consistent with a knowledge base: why enumeration is exponential, the two satisfiability calls that decide entailment, contradiction and contingency (and the proof-by-contradiction reading), model checking with Z3 and its returned model, and SAT solvers (NP-complete in the worst case; DPLL, CDCL).
- **[Inference Rules](wiki/inference-rules.md)** — Deriving formulas by manipulating symbols: modus ponens, rules as premises over a conclusion that act on syntax, forward inference to convergence (Rain → Wet → Slippery), derivation $\vdash$ versus entailment $\models$, soundness and completeness (the water glass: nothing but the truth, the whole truth), checking a rule's soundness with models, and lecture 16's first-order modus ponens on definite clauses (complexity; sound but not complete).
- **[First-Order Logic](wiki/first-order-logic.md)** — Propositional logic plus objects and quantifiers: why propositional logic is clunky, terms (constants, variables, functions) versus formulas (predicates applied to terms, connectives, $\forall$ and $\exists$), common non-formulas such as Knows(Student, arithmetic), models as a domain plus an interpretation of constants, functions and predicates, the recursive interpretation function, the two routes to inference, translating English ($\forall$ with $\rightarrow$, $\exists$ with $\wedge$, nested quantifiers), and higher-order logics.
- **[Propositionalization](wiki/propositionalization.md)** — Reducing a first-order knowledge base to propositional logic: the unique-names and domain-closure assumptions and the three-model figure that illustrates them, atomic formulas as propositional symbols, $\forall$ as a conjunction and $\exists$ as a disjunction over constants, and first-order logic as syntactic sugar.
- **[Substitution and Unification](wiki/unification.md)** — Matching variables for first-order modus ponens: why exact matching fails on Takes(alice, cs221), substitution as recursive search and replace, unification as generalized equality (the three traced examples, including the failure), and modus ponens with $\theta = \text{unify}(\ldots)$ deriving Knows(alice, logic).
- **[Language Models](wiki/language-models.md)** — A distribution over sequences: vocabulary and grammar, the chain rule and autoregression (and lecture 12's Bayesian-network view), next-token prediction as multiclass classification over $V$ classes, tensor shapes, greedy decoding vs. sampling, training on the input shifted by one (cross entropy = maximum likelihood), next-token vs. masked objectives, three implementations (count, n-grams, neural), and why language is worth modeling.
- **[Scaling Laws](wiki/scaling-laws.md)** — Loss falls as a power law in compute, data and parameters (Kaplan et al.'s three fits), Chinchilla's IsoFLOP curves and compute-optimal projection (63B parameters, 1.4T tokens), the 6ND rule and the cost of a Llama 3 run, one model replacing many, GPT-3's accuracy rising with size, and test-time scaling.
- **[Pre-training](wiki/pre-training.md)** — Next-token prediction on a giant amount of text: OLMo 2's data mix (table), what web documents look like, DCLM's filtering from Common Crawl (heuristics, dedup, a model-based filter: 1.4% kept), GPT-3's emergent capabilities and few-shot in-context learning, BERT's masked LM, and why a base model is only "autocomplete on steroids"; and lecture 19's account of where pre-training data comes from and what it costs.
- **[Post-training](wiki/post-training.md)** — Making a base model useful: instruction following and InstructGPT's three RLHF steps (SFT, a reward model from human preferences, RL treating the LM as a policy with policy gradient), safety tuning, jailbreaks (past tense, grandma, leetspeak and base64; Wei et al.'s table), and the techniques SFT, RLHF, RLVR and data curation.
- **[Tokenization](wiki/tokenization.md)** — From strings to token IDs: lecture 3's space splitting and `Vocabulary` class, lecture 17's subword units and byte-pair encoding (start from characters, merge the most frequent pair, stop at the vocabulary size), the vocabulary size $V$ in a model's shapes, and the problems tokenization causes.
- **[Societal Impact of AI](wiki/societal-impact.md)** — Lecture 18's frameworks for orienting oneself, which lecture 19 extends into supply chains and economics: why technologists should care, why principles alone are not enough, dual use technology (ammonia, rockets, nuclear, hacking tools, encryption), the intent × impact grid (benefits, misuse, accidents, and the empty supervillain quadrant), the upstream/downstream ecosystem view, and auditing; with lecture 1's sociotechnical framing.
- **[Inequality in AI](wiki/ai-inequality.md)** — Systems that work better for some groups: Gender Shades (accuracy by skin type and gender for three classifiers) and the power of third-party auditing, more data vs. upweighting vs. distributionally robust optimization (maximize the worst group's accuracy), Starling 7B's reward map by country, and spurious correlations (the chest drain); the lesson to monitor multiple metrics; and lecture 19's uneven effects on workers by age and tenure.
- **[Alignment](wiki/alignment.md)** — Making AI do what we want, framed by the RL recipe (define a reward, maximize its expectation): reward hacking (CoastRunners, coding agents and unit tests) and overoptimization, pluralism (Overton, steerable and distributional models), scalable oversight (decomposition, debate, constitutional AI, process supervision); lecture 1's definition and RLHF's reward models.
- **[Copyright and Fair Use](wiki/copyright-and-fair-use.md)** — Copyright as lecture 18 presents it: intellectual property's purpose, original works, expression vs. ideas, fixed vs. published, registration and expiry; licenses and Creative Commons; the four fair-use factors and examples; the arguments for and against training as fair use; terms of service; memorization (`p(book[i] | book[1:i-1])`) versus extraction; and lecture 19's reading of the Anthropic settlement as a price for data.
- **[Openness and Transparency](wiki/openness-and-transparency.md)** — Centralization of power and the two responses: transparency (the Foundation Model Transparency Index, its 100 indicators, May 2024 scores and the October 2023 comparison) and openness (closed, open-weight, open-source, open-development; open weights as a binary executable), with the benefits of open models and how to weigh their risk (marginal risk, the whole ecosystem); and lecture 19's seven-level access table and what the release choice does downstream.
- **[AI Supply Chain](wiki/ai-supply-chain.md)** — Lecture 19's description of the chain from data and compute to affected persons, seen as technology and as organizations: why capability alone does not say who wins; the layers; compute (ASML, TSMC, Nvidia, monopolies, resilience, geopolitics, clouds, data centers); data (six acquisition methods with their prices, crawling restrictions, the law, running out of data); distribution and its downstream effects.
- **[General-Purpose Technologies](wiki/general-purpose-technologies.md)** — From "normal" to "generic" to general-purpose technologies (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg); the three characteristics and the evidence that AI meets them (cross-sector usage, METR task lengths, Epoch AI's falling prices, technological and organizational complements); the productivity J-curve and why it is slow; the Solow paradox.
- **[Economic Growth and AI](wiki/economic-growth.md)** — How big AI's impact will be (the technological Richter scale, LEAP, forecasts from Acemoglu to utopia and doom); GDP and its 2% a year trend; why free technologies do not show up and GDP-B; the production function $Y = A \cdot K^{\alpha} \cdot L^{1-\alpha}$; three views of AI (one productive sector and Baumol's cost disease, cheap labor, new ideas and Romer).
